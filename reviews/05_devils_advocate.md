# Devil's Advocate Report

Target: `paper/cascade-mis.tex` (1201 lines), audited against `results/*.csv`,
`paper/tables/*.tex`, `scripts/*`, `src/*`. Every number below was recomputed
from the raw CSVs, not read from the prose or the tables.

---

## 1. The central claim, restated fairly

*An exactly-solved large neighbourhood — release a set F from the incumbent, take
the provably separated region it induces, kernelize it, solve it optimally —
yields a monotone move that is cheap enough for an inner loop, and a solver built
on it matches or beats the published state of the art on massive sparse graphs,
with a consistent advantage where the kernel stays large.*

To the paper's credit, it states its own narrowest reading in §Threats ("How
narrow the positive result is") and in §Aggregate ("The honest reading is not
that Cascade dominates ReduMIS..."). Those paragraphs are the best writing in the
manuscript. My job is to show that the rest of the paper does not live up to them
and that the data are weaker than even that self-criticism admits.

---

## 2. The strongest case against it

**(a) The positive result is n = 2, not n = 3, and it is on graphs the authors
made.** Strict wins over all seven solvers: `del16` (+7), `del18` (+77), `del20`
(+264). All three are Delaunay triangulations generated by
`tools/gen_instances.py` at seed 1. The `del16` win does not survive replication:
across the five-seed study Cascade returns {20642, 20642, 20645, 20645, 20647}
and FastVC returns {20633, 20635, 20637, 20638, **20642**}. Cascade's *worst*
seed equals FastVC's *best*. Worse, the same seed (seed 1, 60 s, same machine)
gives Cascade 20647, 20647, 20648, 20648, 20650 and 20654 across the six result
files in `results/` — a 7-vertex same-seed spread, exactly the size of the
claimed 7-vertex margin. So one of the three wins is noise. The defensible count
is **two strict wins out of 27**, both on self-generated planar meshes.

**(b) Every strict win is on a *planar, degree-≤22* graph, and the paper's own
stated mechanism does not predict that.** The paper's explanation is "kernels
stay large". By its own data (`nearlinear` kernel sizes in the CSV notes), the
families with the largest kernels are Delaunay (93–94 % of n) **and BHOSLIB
(100 % of n)** — and on BHOSLIB Cascade loses 6 of 7. The rgg family, which the
Introduction names as a canonical large-kernel case, in fact reduces to 15 % of n
and Cascade only ties there. So "large kernel" has zero predictive power across
the benchmark: it is true of the family Cascade wins on and equally true of the
family it loses on. The variable that actually separates the three wins from
everything else is *planarity plus bounded degree* (max degree 20/21/22 vs
38 625 on web-Stanford, 84 230 on web-BerkStan, hundreds to hundred-thousands
elsewhere). The paper never tests, mentions, or excludes this.

**(c) The statistical test the paper says it ran is not the test it ran, and the
test it says it ran does not support the claim.** The abstract and §Threats both
state the Wilcoxon is "computed over relative gaps". `scripts/stats_test.py`
calls `wilcoxon(ours, other)` on **raw vertex counts**. Recomputed on actual
relative gaps:

| baseline | p (raw sizes, as run & tabulated) | p (relative gaps, as claimed) |
|---|---|---|
| ReduMIS | 0.195 | 0.127 |
| FastVC | **0.030** | **0.441** |
| NuMVC | **0.0008** | **0.155** |
| NearLinear | 0.00014 | 0.00005 |
| OnlineMIS | 0.0000097 | 0.0000098 |
| LinearTime | 0.000034 | 0.000020 |

Under the test the paper *claims* to have run, "significantly better than five of
the six baselines, including both solvers from the vertex cover local search
line" becomes **significantly better than three of six, and neither of the vertex
cover solvers.** The three survivors are OnlineMIS and the two reduction-only
peeling baselines — and 54 of the peeling runs (100 % of `nearlinear` and
`lineartime` rows) are flagged `verified=0` in the CSV. So the only surviving
significant results are against one weak local search and two unverifiable
baselines.

**(d) The main-text tables silently delete the two baselines that beat Cascade.**
`scripts/make_tables.py` line 9 hardcodes `SOLVERS = [cascade, redumis,
online_mis, nearlinear, lineartime]`. NuMVC and FastVC appear in **none** of the
five per-family quality tables, **none** of the aggregate table, and **none** of
the budget table. They appear only in the 189-row appendix longtable. The effect
is uniformly favourable and is not marginal:

- Table 3 (geometric) shows Cascade 330 066 on `del20` against a next-best of
  324 619, implying a margin of **+5 447**. The true margin over the actual
  strongest baseline (FastVC, 329 802) is **+264** — a factor of 20.
- Table 7 (BHOSLIB) prints Cascade's 43 on `frb45-21-1` in **bold as the best
  value**. The actual best is 45 (FastVC and NuMVC). The caption reads "Best
  value per instance in bold." It is wrong.
- Table 5 (social) bolds NearLinear's 789 161 on `soc-pokec` as best. The actual
  best is FastVC's 789 352.
- Table 8's `best found by any algorithm` denominator is computed over the same
  five solvers, which is how Cascade's mean gap comes out at 0.414 % instead of
  0.785 %.

**(e) On the paper's own aggregate metric, Cascade loses.** Table 8 as printed:
Cascade #best 17, mean gap 0.414 %; ReduMIS #best 23, mean gap 0.113 %. Over all
seven solvers: Cascade #best 16, mean gap 0.785 %; ReduMIS 19 / 0.501 %; **FastVC
11 / 0.340 %, the lowest mean gap of any solver in the study.** The paper
introduces this table as the fair way to aggregate ("counting wins is misleading
here... We therefore report both the mean relative gap and a paired Wilcoxon
test") and then never reports that it loses on it. Not one sentence in the
manuscript states that ReduMIS has a lower mean gap and more best values than
Cascade, or that FastVC has the lowest mean gap of all.

**(f) The del20 advantage is a convergence-rate artefact and the paper's own
rebuttal is n = 1.** 60 s → 600 s: ReduMIS gains 7 417 (13.7 v/s over the extra
540 s), NuMVC 6 227, Cascade 467 (0.86 v/s), FastVC 390. The gap to ReduMIS falls
8 500 → 1 550. The paper rescues the claim by switching reference to "the
strongest baseline", which at 600 s is FastVC (330 192, margin 341). But
"strongest baseline" is a running maximum over a set whose fastest-improving
member has not yet arrived; ReduMIS closed 82 % of an 8 500-vertex gap in one
decade of budget and is still improving 16× faster than Cascade. A third budget
point would settle it and was not run. Meanwhile on `roadNet-CA` and
`web-BerkStan` ReduMIS reaches its final value in 69 s and 67 s and matches or
beats Cascade's 600-second answer — a **9× time advantage** that the paper
reports in one paragraph and then omits from every summary.

**(g) The engineering story is entirely unmeasured.** The claims "collapse a
region of thousands of vertices to a kernel of a few dozen", "a move costs about
half a millisecond", "some 1800 of them per second", "107 238 region moves on
del18", "96 842 on web-Stanford", "kernelizes in 1.16 s", "first dive 0.37 s
later", "soc-pokec takes 180.5 s to a fixed point", "cut off at 18.0 s",
"kernelization takes 0.84 s rather than 1.05 s ... 13.98 rather than 48.84" —
none of these is in any distributed artefact. `src/main.cpp` prints `kernel_n`,
`kernel_truncated`, `lns_moves`, `first_dive_time` to stdout, and
`scripts/run_bench.py` discards all of it: the `note` column for every Cascade
row is literally `maximal=yes`. The paper points readers at "the run records
distributed with the source" three separate times for exactly these quantities
and those records do not exist in the repository. Worse, `lns_region_vertices`
*is* accumulated in `CascadeStats` but is **not in the printf**, so the
headline "thousands → a few dozen" claim has no emitted measurement anywhere in
the shipped code.

**(h) The mechanism's key premise is contradicted by the paper's own exact
table.** §Exact argues the sub-solver is fast on "sub-problems of exactly this
shape". It demonstrates this only on `ca-AstroPh`, `ca-CondMat` and
`email-Enron` — three instances that reduce to a kernel of **zero** under
near-linear kernelization, i.e. instances with no sub-problem at all. On the
geometric instances, where regions actually have structure, `cascade-exact`
times out at 60 s on `rgg16` (VCSolver 0.7 s, PACE 0.4 s) and returns 64 271 vs
the proven 64 278 on `rgg18` (VCSolver 2.3 s, PACE 2.2 s) — 85× to 165× *worse*.
The paper reports its exact solver's weakness on BHOSLIB and is silent about its
weakness on the very family the region move is sold on.

**Synthesis of the counter-case.** Strip the presentational choices and what
remains is: a method that ties ReduMIS on most of a 27-instance benchmark, loses
to it on web graphs, loses to FastVC/NuMVC on all of BHOSLIB and on soc-pokec,
has a lower aggregate score than either ReduMIS or FastVC, is separated from
ReduMIS by no test in the paper, and is strictly ahead of everything on two
author-generated planar meshes — where the lead over the nearest rival is 0.09 %
and 0.08 %, is reached 9× slower than ReduMIS reaches parity on other families,
and shrinks 5× against ReduMIS when the clock is loosened. The correct headline
is "competitive with ReduMIS, with a small reproducible edge on planar meshes",
not "beats or matches the state of the art".

## 3. What evidence would defeat this counter-argument

I commit to these in advance; any one of them materially weakens my case, and all
of them together would defeat it.

1. **The same win on archive Delaunay graphs.** Run `delaunay_n16`…`delaunay_n24`
   from the DIMACS10 / SuiteSparse collection — the *public* graphs of the same
   names that Lamm et al. and Akiba–Iwata report on. If Cascade beats FastVC and
   ReduMIS on those by a comparable relative margin, "generator artefact" and
   "family they chose and generated" both die. This costs a day and is the single
   highest-value experiment the paper is missing.
2. **A non-generated large-kernel family.** Walshaw/mesh graphs, `europe.osm`,
   `road_usa`, 3D FEM meshes, or the Delaunay-like triangulations from any
   graph-partitioning archive. A win on a mesh nobody in this project produced
   would convert the result from n = 2 to a family claim.
3. **A non-planar, high-degree, large-kernel instance where the move wins.** This
   would break my "it's planarity + bounded degree" alternative and rescue the
   paper's own "large kernel" mechanism.
4. **A third budget point (3 600 s) on del20 showing ReduMIS's gain rate
   collapsing while Cascade's lead holds.** That converts (f) from "artefact" to
   "asymptotic advantage".
5. **The Wilcoxon actually computed on relative gaps, with FastVC and NuMVC
   significant.** If the relative-gap test came out significant against the
   vertex cover line I would withdraw (c) entirely.
6. **Regenerated tables including all seven solvers, showing Cascade with the
   lowest mean gap.** If it still loses that comparison, (e) stands regardless.
7. **Per-instance instrumentation dumps** (region sizes, region-kernel sizes,
   moves/s, kernel truncation) committed to `results/`. That kills (g).
8. **Five seeds on `del16` where Cascade's minimum exceeds FastVC's maximum.**
   That restores the third win.

## 4. Cherry-picking audit

**Why these 27, why these five families?** The paper never justifies the set.
The five families include one that the method is explicitly unsuited to
(BHOSLIB, 7 instances, 26 % of the benchmark) and one it wins on (geometric, 6
instances) that it generated itself. Seven of the 27 instances (`ca-AstroPh`,
`ca-CondMat`, `email-Enron`, `com-dblp`, `com-youtube`, `wiki-Talk`, plus
effectively `com-amazon`) reduce to kernel 0 or near-0 and are decided by
kernelization; they contribute six of the thirteen "matches the best" and are
matched by the deterministic `nearlinear` baseline in 0.0–0.7 s. Of the thirteen
ties, **six are reached by ≥4 of the 7 solvers** and five of those by a pure
kernelization baseline. So "matches the best on thirteen" is inflated: roughly
half of those ties are instances where any correct kernelizer wins. Selection is
**favourable-by-construction**.

**Absent instances.** The DIMACS10 `delaunay_n*` graphs — the archive versions of
the paper's *only winning family*, publicly available, used by the very ReduMIS
and branch-and-reduce papers cited — are absent, and replaced with
author-generated substitutes bearing the same naming convention (`del16`,
`del18`, `del20`). §Threats concedes "they are not byte-identical to the
instances of the same name used in earlier papers, so cross-paper comparison of
absolute values on those families is not meaningful". That concession is the
problem, not the mitigation: it makes the paper's only positive result the one
thing no other group can check against its own published numbers. Also absent
from the KaMIS/ReduMIS standard set: `web-Google`, `cit-Patents`,
`soc-LiveJournal1`, `in-2004`, `uk-2002`, `hollywood-2011`, `europe.osm`,
`road_usa`, and the Walshaw mesh collection — i.e. every large-kernel
non-geometric candidate. From DIMACS, the dense clique instances (`C*`,
`MANN_a*`, `keller*`, `brock*`) are absent; given the BHOSLIB result they would
be unfavourable. **Would the absent instances be unfavourable? Almost certainly
yes for the DIMACS dense set, and unknown-but-decisive for the archive Delaunay
set.** Favourable-by-construction: yes.

**The 600 s study on 4 instances.** The paper says "four instances, one per
family". **There are five families.** The omitted one is *social* — which
contains `soc-pokec` (Cascade's largest relative loss on any sparse graph,
−0.062 %, and the instance where ReduMIS failed) and `as-skitter` (−4). The
chosen four are `del20` (its best instance), `roadNet-CA`, `web-BerkStan`,
`frb59-26-1`. There is no statement of whether these were chosen before or after
seeing the 60 s results, and no pre-registration. Given that (i) the stated
selection rule is violated, (ii) the violation removes exactly the family
containing the worst sparse loss, and (iii) the paper's defence of `del20` rests
entirely on this table, the burden is on the authors. Favourable-by-construction:
**plausibly yes, and the authors must state the selection order.**

**Sensitivity on 3 instances.** `del18`, `frb40-19-1`, `web-Stanford`. No road,
no social, and — again — **not `del20`**, the instance carrying the headline. The
comparison baseline chosen for "is this within noise?" is the *family-maximum*
seed spread (0.024 %, which is `del16`'s), not `del18`'s own spread (0.0097 %).
Using the larger of the two available noise floors makes three of four parameters
look "below noise". On `del18`'s own noise floor, `lns_start_free` (0.013 %) is
above it. Favourable-by-construction: **yes, mildly.** Separately, the four
blocks each contain the all-defaults configuration and it returns four different
values (82 655, 82 655, 82 656, 82 659) — so the claim "the default sits at the
top of that curve" rests on a 4-vertex difference smaller than the solver's own
same-seed nondeterminism.

**Convergence figure on 4 instances.** `del18`, `roadNet-PA`, `web-Stanford`,
`frb40-19-1`. Again **not `del20`** — the one instance where the convergence
story is contested and where the 8 500 → 1 550 collapse happens. A convergence
figure that omits the instance whose convergence behaviour is the paper's main
liability is favourable-by-construction. It also omits ReduMIS's `roadNet-CA` /
`web-BerkStan` 69 s / 67 s finishes, which are the sharpest convergence facts in
the whole study and run against the paper.

**Ablation on 7 instances.** No social instance, and **not `del20`**. So the
ablation that "attributes the geometric margin to the exact neighbourhood move"
never tests the instance that produces 8 778 of the 8 651-vertex net advantage.

**One thing that is NOT cherry-picked, and I record it.** `results/heuristic.csv`
is an earlier run of the same protocol in which Cascade scored *better* on 6 of
the 8 instances that differ (`del20` 330 154 vs the reported 330 066; `del18`
82 662 vs 82 656; `frb40-19-1` 40 vs the reported 39). The authors reported the
*later, net −55* run. That is the opposite of run-selection and deserves explicit
credit.

## 5. Confirmation-bias audit (with exact quotes)

**B1 — a null result presented as a property of the world.** Abstract: *"A paired
Wilcoxon test over relative gaps places it ahead of five of the six baselines and
finds no significant difference from ReduMIS."* The framing puts "ahead of five"
first and the failure second, in a subordinate clause, with "no significant
difference" standing in for "we could not beat the one baseline that matters".
§Aggregate does correct this ("The honest reading is not that Cascade dominates
ReduMIS") — but the abstract, which is what gets cited, does not. And per §2(c)
the parenthetical "over relative gaps" is false, and under the stated test the
"five" is three.

**B2 — the margin that fell 5× described as stable.** Abstract: *"A run at ten
times the budget shows that this margin over the best baseline is stable, while a
much larger gap to ReduMIS at the same size is mostly a difference in convergence
rate and closes as the budget grows."* §Long budget: *"its margin over the
strongest baseline is essentially unchanged by the extra time --- 264 vertices at
60 seconds, 341 at 600."* Both are literally true and both are achieved by
re-anchoring to a moving maximum over a solver set. The absolute fact — 8 500 →
1 550, an 82 % collapse, with ReduMIS still gaining 16× faster at the end of the
window — is stated once, in the body, and never reaches the abstract or the
conclusions.

**B3 — losses measured in the smallest available unit.** Abstract: *"and is
behind on eleven: by at most $0.06\%$ on five large sparse graphs, and by one or
two vertices on six BHOSLIB instances."* The worst of those five is
486/789 352 = **0.0616 %**, which is not "at most 0.06 %". The BHOSLIB losses are
expressed in vertices (1–2) where they are small, while the geometric wins are
expressed in percent (0.08 %) where *they* are small — each family is reported in
whichever unit flatters. Expressed consistently, the BHOSLIB losses are 2.9–4.4 %
and the geometric wins are 0.03–0.09 %.

**B4 — necessary-for-parity sold as source-of-superiority.** §Ablation: *"The
region move is the component that carries the result. Removing it costs $1.2\%$
on \texttt{del18} ... an order of magnitude more than the margin the full solver
holds over the strongest baseline on those instances."* True, and it proves the
opposite of what it is used for: without LNS, `del18` = 81 639, which is **940
vertices *below*** the best baseline (82 579). The move buys 1 017 vertices, of
which 940 is catching up to the field and 77 is the lead. The sentence "carries
the result" conflates "the solver is uncompetitive without it" with "the move is
why we win".

**B5 — a favourable ablation row not reported.** §Ablation reports *"Removing
perturbation changes the summed total by $+2$ vertices, and hurts only
\texttt{frb40-19-1}, by two."* It does not report that on `del16` the no-perturb
variant returns **20 656** against the full solver's 20 648 and the main table's
20 647 — i.e. **+16 over the best baseline**, more than double the headline +7.
An ablation variant of their own solver outscores the headline configuration by
more than the headline margin, on the headline family. That fact is in
`results/ablation.csv` and appears nowhere in the paper.

**B6 — the aggregate loss never stated.** §Aggregate introduces the mean relative
gap as the principled measure and then discusses only the Wilcoxon and the
per-family decomposition. Cascade's mean gap (0.414 %) is 3.7× ReduMIS's
(0.113 %) *in the paper's own printed table*, and Cascade has 17 #best against
ReduMIS's 23. No sentence anywhere acknowledges this.

**B7 — a universality claim contradicted by the data.** Abstract and
contribution 4: *"with every reported solution independently verified"*. In
`cor_main60.csv`, `verified=0` for **all 27 `nearlinear` rows and all 27
`lineartime` rows** — 54 of 189 values, 28.6 % of the main table. §Threats admits
it for "reducing-peeling" only, singular. Two of the six baselines are entirely
unverified, and they are two of the three the method still significantly beats
under the relative-gap test.

**B8 — a mechanism asserted where the paper's own data contradict it.**
§Introduction: *"Delaunay triangulations and random geometric graphs are the
canonical example, retaining kernels that are a large fraction of the input."*
Measured: rgg kernels are 15.5 % and 14.6 % of n. §Quality then says the
opposite: *"On the random geometric graphs the reduction rules are far more
effective."* The Introduction's framing survives into the reader's model of why
the method works.

**B9 — a weak replication claim dressed as a strong one.** §Replication: *"on all
25 instances, the set of solvers attaining the best value on the replication
machine intersects the set attaining it on the original machine. No solver
changes place with another."* "Intersects" is nearly unfalsifiable. Recomputed,
the best-set is **identical on only 20 of 25**: ReduMIS drops out on
`roadNet-CA`, FastVC drops out on `frb45-21-1`, ReduMIS joins on `frb50-23-1`,
Cascade joins on `frb40-19-1`, NuMVC joins on `email-Enron`. "No solver changes
place with another" is not operationalised and is not true under any natural
reading.

**B10 — a leftover draft marker.** Line 48: `%% TODO: tighten once the final
experiments are in.` The abstract was written before the experiments it
summarises.

## 6. Logic chain: where it breaks

The chain: reductions shrink the region kernel → the exact sub-solve is cheap →
each move is monotone → high move throughput → better final solutions.

**Break 1 — the move/algorithm promotion (the central fallacy).** Theorem 1 and
Corollary 1 are correct and prove a property of a *single move*: it never
decreases |S|. §Positioning converts this into an algorithmic virtue: *"the move
is unconditionally admissible and monotone; no acceptance criterion, tabu list or
repair step is required."* This is false of the algorithm as implemented and as
described four pages later. Algorithm 1 line 10 accepts an equal-size region
optimum *"on a plateau"* — that is an acceptance criterion. §Escaping local
optima describes perturbation *"with a change log that rolls back only the
touched vertices if the result is worse"* — that is a repair step plus a
rejection rule. `src/cascade.cpp:270` (`if (gain > 0 || (changed && plateau_))`)
implements exactly the acceptance criterion the paper says is unnecessary.
Monotonicity is precisely what makes the algorithm get stuck (the paper concedes
this: *"the search stalls at a solution that is optimal for every region of the
current size"*), so all three escape mechanisms exist to *break* the property
that is sold as the contribution. Monotonicity of individual moves implies
nothing whatever about final quality — a solver that returns its greedy dive
unchanged is also monotone.

**Break 2 — throughput is not search progress.** §Implementation: *"the
throughput, not the asymptotics, is what makes an exactly-solved neighbourhood
practical."* 107 238 moves on `del18` produce 1 016 vertices of improvement: one
vertex per 106 moves; ≈99 % of moves are no-ops. `web-Stanford` records **six**
trace points in 60 s against ≈96 842 claimed moves. High throughput here is
evidence that the neighbourhood is mostly barren, not that the search is
effective. The stats struct tracks `lns_improvements` and `lns_gain` — the ratio
that would settle this — and `main.cpp` prints them, and `run_bench.py` throws
them away.

**Break 3 — the cheapness premise is demonstrated only where it is vacuous.** The
"exact sub-solve is affordable" step is evidenced by Table `exact` on three
instances whose kernel is **0**. On the instances where regions have structure
(`rgg16`, `rgg18`), the same sub-solver is 85–165× slower than VCSolver and PACE
and proves nothing. The claim "the sub-problems it generates are of exactly this
shape" is an assertion about an unmeasured distribution.

**Break 4 — "collapse a region of thousands to a kernel of a few dozen" is an
unsupported premise, not a finding.** It is stated in the abstract, the
introduction and §Move, and is measured nowhere. `lns_region_vertices` is
accumulated and never emitted.

**Break 5 — the strict-generalisation argument is about expressiveness, not
value.** Corollary 2 and Figure 2 prove the neighbourhood is strictly larger than
(1,k)-swap. A larger neighbourhood costs more per move and is only worth it if
the extra moves are found. No experiment isolates "improvements attributable to
|F| > 1". The `no-lns` ablation removes the move *entirely*, which cannot
distinguish "large F helps" from "any swap neighbourhood helps"; the natural
control — Cascade restricted to |F| = 1, i.e. classical (1,2)-swap — is exactly
what ReduMIS already is, and the paper is not significantly better than ReduMIS.

## 7. Overgeneralisation inventory

1. **Title**: *"on Massive Sparse Graphs"*. The wins are on 65 K–1 M vertex
   generated meshes. On the genuinely massive instances (`com-lj` 4.0 M,
   `wiki-Talk` 2.4 M, `as-skitter` 1.7 M, `soc-pokec` 1.6 M, `roadNet-CA` 2.0 M)
   the method ties (4), loses (2) and never wins. The title generalises from the
   family where the method is weakest-differentiated.
2. **Abstract**: *"all of them are weak in the same place: instances whose reduced
   graph, or kernel, stays large."* Asserted; the benchmark contains two
   large-kernel families and the method wins on one and loses on the other.
3. **Abstract**: *"a move costs about half a millisecond and the solver performs
   some 1800 of them per second."* From two instances; 0.5 ms and 1800/s are
   mutually inconsistent (0.5 ms ⇒ 2000/s) and the measured values are 543 µs
   (1 842/s) and 605 µs (1 653/s).
4. **Abstract**: *"a margin that grows with size, reaching $0.08\%$ ... at one
   million vertices."* The *relative* margin is 0.034 %, 0.093 %, **0.080 %** — it
   peaks at del18 and *falls* at del20. "Grows with size, reaching 0.08 %" reads
   as a culmination; it is a decline.
5. **Introduction**: *"the conflict graphs arising from real data routinely have
   millions of vertices and average degree in the low tens, which is the regime
   this paper addresses."* The three wins are on graphs of average degree 6.0 and
   at most 1 M vertices. The instances that *do* match this description are the
   ones where the method ties or loses.
6. **Introduction, applications**: scheduling, wireless interference, map
   labelling, computer vision, entity resolution. **Not one instance in the
   benchmark comes from any of these domains.** Delaunay triangulations are a
   mesh-partitioning artefact; road/web/social graphs are not conflict graphs.
   Zero evidence is offered for any of the five motivations. If the honest claim
   is "planar meshes", the applications paragraph should say so or go.
7. **Introduction**: *"Local search methods ... notably NuMVC and FastVC ...
   either fail outright or lose ground on graphs with millions of vertices."*
   FastVC has the lowest mean relative gap of all seven solvers over this
   benchmark (0.340 %) and is the strongest baseline on `del18`, `del20` and
   `soc-pokec`. It does not fail and does not lose ground; it wins the aggregate.
8. **§Positioning**: *"one configuration to cover instance families whose
   appropriate neighbourhood sizes differ by three orders of magnitude."* No
   measurement of realised k is reported for any instance. `lns_target` is in the
   stats struct and is not printed.
9. **§Positioning**: *"our ablation shows that the move carries essentially the
   entire improvement over the baselines."* The ablation shows the move carries
   the improvement over *no move*. See Break 1 / B4.
10. **§Conclusions**: *"the ablation confirms that the move is where the
    improvement comes from: removing it costs more than the entire margin over
    the baselines."* Same conflation, now stated as a confirmed finding.
11. **§Conclusions**: *"The region definition and Theorem 1 carry over verbatim
    to the weighted problem by replacing cardinality with weight."* The theorem
    does; Corollary 1's monotonicity does (max weight ≥ weight of F); but the
    *adaptive-k rule*, the plateau criterion and, critically, the reduction rules
    (weighted MIS has a materially different and weaker rule set) do not. "Carry
    over verbatim" overstates, and no weighted experiment exists.
12. **§Replication**: *"A reader can therefore take the comparisons in
    Section 5 as being about the algorithms."* From 25 instances on two machines
    of the same vendor and near-identical clock (2.10 vs 2.20 GHz Xeon), with a
    confounded build. That supports "not a fluke of this box", not "about the
    algorithms".

## 8. Alternative explanations not excluded

**For the Delaunay win specifically:**

- **Generator artefact.** `delaunay_edges` uses `scipy.spatial.Delaunay` (Qhull)
  on `numpy.random.default_rng(1).random((n,2))`. Uniform-in-unit-square points
  give a very specific degree distribution (mean 6, max 20–22) and a specific
  local structure. Whether the win transfers to *any* other Delaunay
  triangulation — DIMACS10's, a mesh from an actual FEM problem, points from a
  non-uniform distribution — is untested. **Not excluded. This is the single
  most important uncontrolled variable in the paper.**
- **Planarity.** All three wins are planar; no other instance in the benchmark
  is. Planar graphs have O(1) average degree, small separators, and — relevantly
  — regions R(F) that are geometrically localised, so the region kernel is small
  *because of planarity*, not because reductions are clever. **Not excluded, not
  mentioned.**
- **Bounded degree.** Max degree 20/21/22 on the wins; 9–12 on roads (small
  kernel, no win); 122–277 on BHOSLIB (large kernel, loss). Bounded degree
  bounds |R(F)| ≤ |F|·Δ, which is exactly what makes the exact sub-solve cheap.
  The mechanism the paper credits (reductions) may be doing nothing that the
  degree bound was not already doing. **Not excluded**, and testable in an hour
  by reporting region sizes with reductions on and off.
- **Baseline default parameters.** ReduMIS's KaMIS defaults (evolutionary
  population size, ILS iteration counts, convergence thresholds) were tuned on
  social/web graphs. On `del20` ReduMIS is still gaining 13.7 v/s at t = 600 s —
  a textbook symptom of under-tuned restart/population settings, not of a worse
  algorithm. No baseline tuning was attempted; no baseline was given a warm
  start. **Not excluded**, and it is the alternative that would most damage the
  headline.
- **Budget interacting with construction cost.** Cascade's dive reproduces
  reducing-peeling and lands at 81 639 on `del18` within 1.7 s. Its entire
  advantage over ReduMIS on the geometric family may be "starts from a better
  greedy", not "has a better neighbourhood". The `no-lns` ablation (81 639 =
  exactly the dive value) is consistent with this and does not separate it.
  **Not excluded.**

**For the other findings:**

- *Ties on social/road/web.* Alternative: these instances are decided by
  kernelization and **all** the ties are ties of the kernelizer, not of the
  search. Supported: `nearlinear` alone matches the best on 6 of the 13 ties in
  0.0–0.7 s. Not excluded.
- *Losses on web graphs (−15, −102).* The paper says "we have not identified a
  structural reason". Obvious candidate it does not consider: `web-Stanford` and
  `web-BerkStan` have max degree 38 625 and 84 230, so a region touching a hub
  explodes and the adaptive rule drives k to 1 — i.e. exactly the BHOSLIB
  degeneracy in a sparse graph. That would unify the two failure modes and
  contradict the "dense graph" framing. Untested.
- *BHOSLIB losses.* The paper attributes these structurally. Alternative: they
  are implementation immaturity in the sub-solver — `cascade-exact` returns 25,
  28, 30 against true 30, 35, 40 where PACE proves all three in under 4 s. A
  sub-solver that weak on the family cannot support a structural claim.
- *The 1.25×–3.5× kernelization speedup from degree-ordered queue seeding.* Three
  data points, one run each, no variance, no artefact. The "grows faster than the
  graph does" inference is drawn from n = 3.
- *The `restart_idle_dives` "sensitivity".* Alternative: nondeterminism. The
  all-defaults configuration returns 82 655/82 655/82 656/82 659 across the four
  blocks; the claimed "top of the curve" is 4 vertices.
- *Replication stability.* Alternative: the two machines are the same
  microarchitecture at the same clock, so of course they agree.

## 9. Stakeholder blind spots

**The practitioner with a non-geometric graph.** The title, abstract and
conclusions say "massive sparse graphs". A practitioner with a 2 M-vertex entity
resolution graph (one of the paper's own motivating applications, average degree
in the tens, hub structure) reads this and adopts Cascade. From this paper's own
data they get: the same answer as ReduMIS, obtained 9× slower (`roadNet-CA`: 603 s
vs 69 s), or 0.025 % worse (`web-BerkStan`), or 0.06 % worse than FastVC
(`soc-pokec`). The honest advice for them is "run near-linear kernelization first;
if the kernel is small, use ReduMIS and stop." The paper contains that advice
(§Long budget, one paragraph) and contradicts it in the title.

**The practitioner with a dense graph.** Reasonably well served: §Quality,
§Seeds, §Threats and §Conclusions all say plainly that the move degenerates and
NuMVC/FastVC are better. The seed study's warning about single-seed variance is
genuinely useful. Credit where due.

**The reviewer of a later paper.** Someone cites "0.08 % over the strongest
baseline" as the state-of-the-art margin on Delaunay graphs and uses it to reject
a submission reporting 0.05 %. They will not be able to reproduce it: the
instances are not the archive `delaunay_n*` graphs, the comparison solver
(FastVC) does not appear in any table in the paper, and §Threats says
cross-paper comparison on that family is meaningless. A margin that cannot be
cited is a margin that should not be in an abstract.

**The reader who reads only the tables.** Gets a strictly false picture:
`del20` margin appears as +5 447 instead of +264; Cascade appears to hold the
best value on `frb45-21-1` when it is 2 short; the two solvers that beat it most
often are invisible.

**The reimplementer.** §Ablation honestly says perturbation and the LP reduction
have not been shown to pay for themselves — excellent. But they cannot reproduce
the throughput, region-size, kernel-size or truncation claims, because the data
are discarded by `run_bench.py` despite being printed by `main.cpp`. And they
cannot rebuild the benchmark from Table A.1, which labels two SNAP graphs
(`soc-pokec-relationships`, `com-lj`) as "generated‡ by `tools/gen_instances.py`
at seed 1" — a generator that emits only `del*` and `rgg*`.

**The field.** The real cost of publishing as written is the precedent: a
method whose only strict wins are on instances it generated with its own tool,
whose main-text tables omit the baselines that beat it, and whose headline
statistical test is not the test it claims. Each of those is individually
fixable; together they are a template.

## 10. The "so what?" verdict

**Suppose every number is correct.** Then:

*What genuinely changes.* (i) There is a clean, correct theorem — Theorem 1 with
Corollary 1 — identifying an incumbent-defined region that is provably separated
from the rest of the solution. It is simple, it is right, it generalises
(1,2)-swap exactly, and it is a legitimately reusable primitive that carries to
the weighted case. (ii) The engineering claim that reduction rules are cheap
enough to run *inside* a search loop — if it were actually measured — would be a
real contribution, because the whole field currently treats kernelization as a
preprocessing phase. (iii) Three genuinely useful negative results are reported:
the decision-space move is worthless, perturbation does not pay, the LP reduction
does not pay. Honest negative results are rare and valuable. (iv) The BHOSLIB
clique-vs-complement warning in §Instances and Appendix A is a real service to
the community — "every solver we tested returns exactly 15" is a trap other
papers have fallen into.

*What does not change.* No practitioner changes solver. On 24 of 27 instances the
recommendation is unchanged — ReduMIS if the kernel reduces, NuMVC/FastVC if the
graph is dense. On the 3 where it changes, the change is worth 0.03–0.09 % on
graphs the authors generated. No benchmark record falls. No theoretical barrier
moves. The 0.08 % is not a number anyone should build on.

**Verdict:** the contribution is a *primitive and a negative-results paper*
wearing the costume of a state-of-the-art paper. The costume is the problem. If
the theorem, the in-loop-reduction engineering (properly instrumented), the
negative ablations and a *measured* claim of parity-with-ReduMIS-plus-an-edge-on-
planar-meshes were the paper, I would recommend acceptance without much argument.
As written, the abstract promises something the data do not deliver and the
tables actively obscure that.

**Major revision — and only if the revision changes what the paper claims, not
how it argues for the current claim.**

Four things are worth publishing: Theorem 1 with Corollary 1; in-move
kernelization as an engineering idea; three honest negative ablations; and the
BHOSLIB clique-complement warning. Nothing in my audit touches the theorem —
I checked the proofs of Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Lemmas 1–3 and Proposition 1 and
they are correct. The problem is entirely between the evidence and the claim.

**Mandatory before this can be assessed again:**

1. **Regenerate every table with all seven solvers.** This is a one-line fix to
   `make_tables.py:9` and it corrects two false "best value" boldings, a 20×
   overstated visual margin, and the aggregate denominator. Nothing else in the
   review matters until the tables show the data. (C1)

   report the result — including FastVC p = 0.441 and NuMVC p = 0.155 — or state

7. **Report the aggregate loss.** One sentence in §Aggregate stating that ReduMIS
   and FastVC have lower mean relative gaps. (C8)

   kernel fraction and address planarity explicitly. (C9)
