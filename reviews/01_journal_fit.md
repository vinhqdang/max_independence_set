# Journal-Fit Reviewer Report

**Manuscript:** "Exact Large-Neighbourhood Search for the Maximum Independent Set Problem on Massive Sparse Graphs"
**Seat:** Journal-Fit Reviewer (Associate Editor, metaheuristics / graph optimisation area), *Computers & Operations Research*
**Scope of this seat:** journal fit, originality, significance, relevance to the COR readership, exposition and framing. Statistical and experimental-design adequacy belong to another seat; where I touch them it is because the *text* misdescribes what was done, which is a framing matter.

---

## 1. Summary of the manuscript

The paper proposes a large-neighbourhood move for the maximum independent set problem in which a set `F` of vertices is released from the incumbent and the induced "region" `R(F)` — `F` plus every non-incumbent vertex all of whose incumbent neighbours lie in `F` — is re-solved to optimality. Theorem 1 (l. 340–364) shows `R(F)` is edge-separated from `S \ R(F)`, so the substitution is unconditionally admissible, and Corollary 2 gives monotonicity; with `|F|=1` the move reduces to the classical (1,2)-swap and for larger `F` it expresses moves no swap neighbourhood does. The engineering contribution is that the standard safe reduction rules (Nemhauser–Trotter LP, folding, unconfined, domination) are applied *inside* each move rather than only as preprocessing, which, together with a payload-free undo structure and a reused sub-solver, brings a move down to roughly 0.5 ms and about 1800 moves/second; `|F|` is adapted online from whether sub-problems are being proved optimal. The evaluation covers 27 instances in five families against six published solvers plus exact/IP references at a 60 s budget, with seed, long-budget, sensitivity, ablation and cross-hardware replication studies. The reported outcome is narrow: strictly ahead of every baseline on three instances (all Delaunay, all author-generated), tied on thirteen, behind on eleven, and statistically indistinguishable from ReduMIS.

## 2. Fit with *Computers & Operations Research*

**My assessment: COR is a defensible but not obviously correct venue, and the manuscript as written does very little to earn it.** The problem (maximum stable set / minimum vertex cover / set packing) is unquestionably in COR's scope, and COR does publish MIS/clique work — the paper's own reference `li2017momc` is a COR paper. Large-neighbourhood search with an exactly solved sub-problem is squarely a COR-readership topic. So the *topic* fits.

What does not fit is the paper's **literature base and idiom**. Concretely:

- **Of 28 references, exactly one is in an OR journal** (`li2017momc`, COR 2017). There is no EJOR, no *INFORMS Journal on Computing*, no *Operations Research*, no *Networks*, no *Discrete Applied Mathematics*, no *Annals of OR*, no *Omega*, no *OR Letters*. The remainder are TCS, SICOMP, JACM, J. Algorithms, ALENEX/SEA/ESA/IPEC, SIGMOD, WWW, GECCO, JAIR/IJCAI and *Journal of Heuristics*. This is an algorithm-engineering bibliography with an OR cover sheet.
- **The paper's central claim is a metaheuristic claim and it cites no metaheuristic literature whatsoever.** Section 1.1, "Positioning" (l. 214–216) opens: *"Large-neighbourhood search with an exactly solved sub-problem is a standard metaheuristic pattern"* — and attaches **no citation at all** to that sentence. A grep of `refs.bib` for `neighborhood|neighbourhood|VLSN|LNS|Shaw|Ahuja|Pisinger|tabu|metaheuristic` returns nothing. The canonical antecedents — Ahuja, Ergun, Orlin & Punnen's very-large-scale neighbourhood survey (DAM 2002), Shaw (1998), Pisinger & Røpke on LNS/ALNS, the matheuristic and "exact-subproblem-inside-a-heuristic" literature (local branching, RINS/relaxation-induced neighbourhoods, corridor method) — are exactly where a COR referee will look first, and they are all absent. Theorem 1 is, in VLSN language, an improvement-graph separation argument; the paper does not say so, which makes it impossible for a COR reader to judge how novel it is.
- **The OR relevance of the problem is asserted without a single reference.** The applications paragraph (l. 96–109: scheduling, wireless interference, map labelling, computer vision, entity resolution) contains **zero `\cite` commands**. For COR this is the one paragraph that has to be sourced, and it is the one that is not.
- **The paper mislabels its own baselines to manufacture OR lineage.** Line 119–121: *"Local search methods developed in the operations research literature, notably NuMVC \cite{cai2013numvc} and FastVC \cite{cai2017fastvc}"*. NuMVC is JAIR 2013 and FastVC is IJCAI 2015 / JAIR 2017 — these are AI-community papers, not OR. This is the sole attempt in the manuscript to claim an OR pedigree and it is factually wrong.
- **No OR-native framing of the problem at all.** MIS is a set-packing problem; the natural COR angles — the stable-set polytope, clique/odd-hole inequalities, Balas–Yu, Rebennack et al.'s branch-and-cut tutorial for the maximum stable set problem, column generation for set packing — are not mentioned. The only IP content is "HiGHS on the natural integer program" (l. 906–908), which is then dismissed (l. 936–947). Running the weakest possible edge formulation and concluding "the integer-programming route is not a usable baseline at this scale" is not an engagement with the OR literature; it is a strawman. A COR referee will notice that no clique-cover or clique-inequality strengthening was tried, and that the paper's own sub-solver uses a clique-cover bound internally (l. 522–524) — so the authors know the strengthening exists.

**Versus alternative venues.** As currently written the paper reads as an *ACM Journal of Experimental Algorithmics* / ALENEX / SEA submission: experimental methodology is its strongest suit (verification of every returned set, budget accounting around the whole process, cross-hardware replication, build-confound measurement, instance-provenance appendix including the genuinely useful BHOSLIB clique-vs-complement warning in §5.2 and Appendix A). JEA is the natural home for that and the paper would need almost no reframing to go there; JEA also rewards the honest-negative reporting this paper does well. *EJOR* would demand the same OR-literature engagement COR does and is, if anything, less hospitable to a pure algorithm-engineering result. *INFORMS JoC* is plausible **only** with the OR reframing described in §4 below plus a weighted-MIS extension or an IP-strengthened comparison; IJOC referees will want the matheuristic connection made explicit and will want the LNS to be positioned against local branching / RINS. My honest ranking of venue fit for the manuscript *as submitted*: JEA > COR ≈ EJOR > IJOC. With the reframing in §4 and a repaired bibliography: COR ≈ IJOC > JEA.

## 3. Originality and significance

**Originality — modest but real, and undersold in the wrong direction.** The genuinely new ingredient is not "an LNS with an exact sub-solve" (that pattern is decades old) and not Theorem 1 in isolation (the separation argument is two paragraphs and, once stated, obvious). It is the *combination*: the observation that the incumbent-defined region is exactly the object on which the standard MIS reduction rules are maximally effective, so that a region of thousands of vertices reduces to a kernel of a few dozen and an exact sub-solve becomes an inner-loop operation at ~1800/s. That is a nice idea and, as far as I can tell from the literature the paper cites, has not been published for MIS. The dynamic graph with payload-free LIFO undo (§4.2, l. 503–515) is solid engineering and the degree-ordered reduction queue result (l. 526–538: 3.5× at four million vertices) is a useful, transferable finding.

**Significance — low, and the paper knows it.** Section 5.12's paragraph "How narrow the positive result is" (l. 1110–1125) is an unusually candid statement and it is essentially correct. The contribution is: a move that helps on graphs whose kernel stays large, costs nothing where the kernel vanishes, and is the wrong tool on dense graphs. That is a *boundary result*, and boundary results are publishable — but the paper is not currently written as one.

**The originality claim is also weakened by an unquantified central mechanism.** The paper's key engineering assertion — *"they collapse a region of thousands of vertices to a kernel of a few dozen"* (l. 138, repeated in the abstract, l. 58–60) — is never measured anywhere in the manuscript. There is no table, figure or number giving the distribution of region sizes or region-kernel sizes. I checked the artefacts: `src/cascade.hpp` does maintain `lns_region_vertices` and `lns_nodes` (l. 92–93 of that file), but `src/main.cpp` never prints them, and `results/traces.csv` carries only `(instance, solver, seconds, size)`. So the claim is not in the paper *and* not in the "run records distributed with the source" either. The single most interesting and most original claim in the paper is the one with no evidence behind it. That is a serious missed opportunity, not merely an omission.

## 4. Is the positive result strong enough? — **Direct verdict**

**No. Not as a "beats state of the art" paper. The positive result as presented is not a publishable contribution in COR, and one of the two claims that currently support it does not survive checking.**

Here is the reasoning, with everything verified against `results/cor_main60.csv` and `results/cor_long600.csv`.

**(a) The win is three instances of one author-generated family, and the margin is ~0.03–0.09%.** I recomputed from the CSV: Cascade is strictly ahead of all six baselines on `del16` (+7 over ReduMIS), `del18` (+77 over FastVC) and `del20` (+264 over FastVC) — these match l. 638–641 exactly. In relative terms 0.034%, 0.093%, 0.080%. These are single-seed differences of a few hundredths of a percent on three instances generated by `tools/gen_instances.py` at seed 1, which §A explicitly states are *not* the DIMACS graphs of the same names and are not comparable across papers. A reviewer is entitled to ask: on an instance family of your own construction, is 0.08% a contribution? By itself, no.

**(b) The del20 headline is a convergence-rate artefact and the authors say so.** §5.7 (l. 833–849): the +8500 over ReduMIS at 60 s becomes +1550 at 600 s; ReduMIS gains 7417 vertices from the extra time against Cascade's 467. I verified both numbers in `cor_long600.csv`. The paper's defence — that the margin over the *strongest* baseline (FastVC) is stable at 264 → 341 — is correct but is a defence of a 0.08–0.10% margin, not of a headline.

**(c) On the same long-budget table Cascade loses the argument on efficiency.** ReduMIS reaches 408482 on `web-BerkStan` in 66.9 s and 961851 on `roadNet-CA` in 69.1 s; Cascade takes ~602 s to return 408380 and 961851. The paper states this plainly (l. 850–857) and it is the correct reading: on kernel-collapsing graphs, "kernelize and stop" dominates.

**(d) It is behind on eleven instances, and six of those losses are structural.** Verified: `frb35-17-1` (34 vs 35), `frb40-19-1` (39 vs 40), `frb45-21-1` (43 vs 45), `frb50-23-1` (48 vs 50), `frb53-24-1` (50 vs 52), `frb59-26-1` (56 vs 58), plus `as-skitter`, `roadNet-PA`, `soc-pokec`, `web-Stanford`, `web-BerkStan`.

**(e) — and this is the finding that decides my recommendation — the aggregate evidence is weaker than the paper reports, in two separable ways that both run in the authors' favour.**

**(e1) The main quality tables and the aggregate table silently omit NuMVC and FastVC.** Tables 2–6 and Table 8 (`paper/tables/quality_*.tex`, `aggregate.tex`, `budget.tex`) contain only five solvers: Cascade, ReduMIS, OnlineMIS, NearLinear, LinearTime. The cause is visible in `scripts/make_tables.py` l. 9–11, where `SOLVERS` omits `fastvc` and `numvc`; `make_appendix_tables.py`, `seeds.tex` and `longbudget.tex` do include them. The consequences are not cosmetic:

| quantity | as printed (Table 6) | recomputed over all 7 solvers |
|---|---|---|
| Cascade #best | 17 | **16** |
| Cascade mean gap | 0.414% | **0.785%** |
| Cascade max gap | 2.857% | **4.444%** |
| ReduMIS #best | 23 | 19 |
| FastVC mean gap | *absent* | **0.340%** |
| NuMVC mean gap | *absent* | 1.219% |

On the paper's own headline aggregate statistic — mean relative gap — Cascade is **third of seven**, behind FastVC (0.340%) and ReduMIS (0.501%). Table 6 as printed shows Cascade second of five. Its caption, *"the best found by any algorithm"*, is false as stated. The text contradicts its own tables in at least three places: l. 640 says the strongest baseline on `del18`/`del20` *"is FastVC"* (not in Table 2); l. 667 says on `soc-pokec` Cascade is *"0.06% behind FastVC, the best value there"* while Table 4 bolds NearLinear's 789161 as best; l. 685–690 says the BHOSLIB best value *"is NuMVC's or FastVC's on every one of them — 45 against our 43"* while Table 5 bolds Cascade's own 43 as best on `frb45-21-1`. l. 742 asks the reader to read Table 8 alongside a claim about NuMVC's 204 s mean — NuMVC is not in Table 8. And l. 686–689's *"4.4% relative gap, the largest we record anywhere"* is contradicted by Table 6's max-gap column of 2.857%.

**(e2) The Wilcoxon test is not the test the paper says it is, and under the paper's stated method two of the five "wins" disappear.** The abstract (l. 74–76) says *"A paired Wilcoxon test **over relative gaps** places it ahead of five of the six baselines"*, and §5.12 (l. 1115) repeats *"The Wilcoxon tests in Table 7 are computed over relative gaps"*. `scripts/stats_test.py` runs `wilcoxon(ours, other)` on **raw independent-set sizes**. I reproduced both:

| baseline | p on raw sizes | p in Table 7 | p on relative gaps |
|---|---|---|---|
| ReduMIS | 0.19506 | 0.1951 | 0.12682 |
| FastVC | 0.02998 | **0.0300** | **0.44062** |
| NuMVC | 0.00079 | **0.0008** | **0.15493** |
| NearLinear | 0.00014 | 0.0001 | 0.00005 |
| OnlineMIS | 0.00001 | <1e-4 | 0.00001 |
| LinearTime | 0.00003 | <1e-4 | 0.00002 |

Table 7 matches the **raw-size** column exactly. Under the paper's *own stated* methodology — relative gaps, which §5.4 (l. 705–709) argues for explicitly because *"instances differ in scale by four orders of magnitude"* — Cascade is significantly ahead of **three of six** baselines, not five, and both surviving comparisons against a serious solver (FastVC, NuMVC) evaporate. The three remaining "wins" are against OnlineMIS and the two single-pass peeling/kernelization heuristics, which nobody would field as the strongest available alternative. I flag this as a framing/integrity finding and defer adjudication of *which* test is correct to the statistics seat; but on any reading, the abstract's claim does not match the computation that was run.

**So: what is left?** After (e1) and (e2), the defensible empirical claim is: *Cascade is statistically indistinguishable from ReduMIS and from FastVC over this benchmark; it is measurably better than three weak baselines; it wins by 0.03–0.09% on three self-generated Delaunay instances; it is materially worse on dense instances; and it is an order of magnitude slower than ReduMIS on the graphs that kernelize away.* That is a negative-to-neutral result with one small positive pocket. It does not clear the COR bar as a "new state of the art" paper.

### Is the honesty an asset or does it merely document a negative result?

**Both, and the paper currently gets the worst of both.** The candour of §5.7, §5.9, §5.10, §5.12 and the "Implementation maturity" paragraph (l. 1069–1093) is genuinely admirable and is the best writing in the manuscript — the ablation section reporting that the authors' *first* move design (decision-space moves) is "worthless" (l. 973–979), and that perturbation and the LP reduction do not pay for themselves (l. 981–986), is the kind of reporting the field needs. But honesty about a weak result does not convert it into a strong one. Right now the paper asserts a SOTA claim in the title, abstract and §5.3, and then spends fifteen pages walking it back. A referee reading in order feels misled by the front matter and then reassured by the back matter, which is the worst possible ordering. **And the candour is undercut by (e1) and (e2): the paper cannot simultaneously claim credit for unusual transparency and print aggregate tables that omit the two baselines that beat it and a p-value column computed by a method other than the one described.** Those two defects are, in my judgement, more damaging to the paper's credibility than the weak result itself.

### What reframing would look like, concretely

I think there **is** a publishable COR paper here, but it is not this one. It is a **theory-plus-engineering paper about a neighbourhood and its domain of applicability**, and it would require:

1. **Retitle and re-abstract away from SOTA.** Something on the order of *"An Exactly Solved, Reduction-Accelerated Neighbourhood for Maximum Independent Set: Structure, Cost, and Domain of Applicability."* The abstract should lead with Theorem 1, the reductions-inside-the-move mechanism and the throughput, and should state the empirical boundary (large-kernel graphs yes, kernel-collapsing graphs no gain, dense graphs no) as the *result*, not as a caveat. Delete "attains the best value of all seven solvers" from the abstract's headline position.
2. **Make the mechanism the contribution and measure it.** Report the region-size and region-kernel-size distributions per family (the instrumentation `lns_region_vertices` already exists; add the per-move histogram), the reduction ratio inside the move, the fraction of sub-solves proved optimal, the adapted `k` trajectory over time per family, and the cost breakdown per move. This is the paper's genuinely new claim and it currently has *no* evidence. Two figures here would be worth more than Tables 2–6 combined.
3. **Turn "where does the move help?" into a designed experiment rather than a post-hoc observation.** The paper already has the right hypothesis (kernel fraction predicts benefit, l. 1119–1122) and already concedes *"we do not know of a public collection"* of large-kernel families. Generate one: sweep a parameter that controls kernel fraction (Delaunay/RGG density, planted structures, mesh irregularity) and plot Cascade-minus-best-baseline against measured kernel fraction. A clean monotone relationship over 50–100 generated instances would be a *far* stronger COR contribution than three wins on `del16/18/20`, and it would make the self-generated instances an asset rather than the liability they currently are.
4. **Connect to the OR literature properly.** Position Theorem 1 within VLSN/improvement-graph theory (Ahuja–Ergun–Orlin–Punnen), position the exact-sub-solve-inside-a-heuristic within matheuristics (local branching, RINS, corridor method), cite the stable-set/set-packing OR literature, and cite the applications paragraph. Fix the false attribution of NuMVC/FastVC to OR at l. 119–121.
5. **Repair the tables and the test.** All seven solvers in every quality table, aggregate and budget table; recompute #best, mean gap and max gap over the full pool; state plainly that FastVC has the lowest mean relative gap on this benchmark. Report the Wilcoxon exactly as computed, or recompute on relative gaps and report the resulting three-of-six honestly. Either is survivable; the mismatch is not.
6. **Cut roughly a third of the manuscript** (see §5).

Without at least (1), (2), (5) and (6) the paper is below the COR bar. With them it becomes a solid, modest, honest COR paper about a neighbourhood and where it works.

## 5. Presentation and length

**Quality of the prose: high.** This is well-written, the argument is easy to follow, the threats-to-validity section is a model of its kind, and the Figure 2 worked example of a region move that no swap expresses (l. 373–391) is genuinely illuminating. I want to be clear that the writing is not the problem.

**Length: 42 printed pages (verified via `pdfinfo`), ~9,900 words of body text, 18 tables and 5 figures. This is roughly 12–15 pages too long for the size of the contribution.** Specific excisions:

- **§2 Preliminaries (l. 229–330) reproduces three textbook proofs** — simplicial (Lemma 1), domination (Lemma 2), degree-2 folding (Lemma 3) — for rules the paper itself attributes to `akiba2016`/`xiao2017`/`nemhauser1975`. That is ~2.5 pages of known material. Cite and delete, or move to an appendix.
- **§5 (Computational study) runs l. 565–1125: 560 of 1201 source lines, 47% of the manuscript, in thirteen subsections.** Convergence (§5.6), long budget (§5.7), sensitivity (§5.8) and replication (§5.11) are four separate subsections whose joint conclusion is "the 60 s ranking is not an artefact". They should be one subsection of two pages with the detail in an appendix. §5.8's honest admission that the dense-instance block "runs out of resolution" (l. 887–899) does not need a full page to say.
- **§5.9 (Exact solvers) is largely orthogonal** to the contribution. Its only load-bearing sentence is that the sub-solver is fast on small reduced graphs, and even that rests on three instances at sub-100-ms resolution.
- **Appendix B (`tables/appendix.tex`, ~200 lines, 189 rows)** duplicates the main tables and belongs in the online supplement, not in a 42-page manuscript.

A 26–30 page paper with the theory, the mechanism measurements requested in §4(2), one consolidated experimental section and everything else in supplementary material would be both stronger and more likely to be read.

## 6. Specific issues

1. **[CRITICAL] Tables 2–6 and 8 omit NuMVC and FastVC; the aggregate statistics computed from them systematically flatter the proposed method.** `paper/tables/quality_geometric.tex`, `quality_road.tex`, `quality_social.tex`, `quality_web.tex`, `quality_bhoslib.tex`, `aggregate.tex`, `budget.tex` list five solvers; `results/cor_main60.csv` has seven; the cause is `scripts/make_tables.py` l. 9–11. Recomputed over all seven: Cascade's mean gap is 0.785% (printed 0.414%), max gap 4.444% (printed 2.857%), #best 16 (printed 17), and **FastVC has the lowest mean gap of any solver at 0.340%** — a fact the manuscript nowhere states. *Remedy:* add `fastvc` and `numvc` to `SOLVERS`, regenerate all tables, re-bold every best cell, recompute Table 6 in full, and add one sentence to §5.4 stating Cascade's true rank on mean relative gap.

2. **[CRITICAL] The Wilcoxon test described in the text is not the test that was run, and the abstract's headline claim does not survive the described method.** Abstract l. 74–76 and §5.12 l. 1115 both say the test is *"over relative gaps"*; `scripts/stats_test.py` calls `wilcoxon(ours, other)` on raw sizes, and Table 7's p-values match raw sizes to four decimals. Recomputed on relative gaps: FastVC 0.0300 → 0.4406 and NuMVC 0.0008 → 0.1549, i.e. *"ahead of five of the six baselines"* becomes ahead of three, all three of them weak baselines. *Remedy:* decide which statistic is intended, run that one, report it, and rewrite the abstract sentence and §5.12 to match. If raw sizes are kept, §5.4 l. 705–709 must stop arguing that scale-free comparison is necessary, since the test then does exactly what that paragraph says is misleading. Defer the choice-of-test question to the statistics reviewer; the mismatch itself is not defensible either way.

3. **[CRITICAL] Line 48: leftover draft marker in the abstract — `%% TODO: tighten once the final experiments are in.`** This is the only `TODO`/`FIXME` in the manuscript (I grepped `cascade-mis.tex` and all of `paper/tables/*.tex`), but in the abstract it is the worst possible place for it and signals the submission was not proofread. *Remedy:* delete, and act on it — the abstract is 31 lines / ~330 words, roughly double the COR norm, and tries to state seven results at once.

4. **[MAJOR] The paper's single most original claim is unmeasured.** L. 138 and abstract l. 58–60: *"they collapse a region of thousands of vertices to a kernel of a few dozen."* No table, figure or number in the manuscript supports this. `src/cascade.hpp` l. 92–93 maintains `lns_region_vertices` and `lns_nodes`, but `src/main.cpp` (l. 116–124) does not print them and `results/traces.csv` carries only `(instance, solver, seconds, size)` — so the claim is not in the "run records distributed with the source" (l. 476) either. *Remedy:* print the statistic, and add a per-family table or figure of region size, region-kernel size, reduction ratio, and fraction of sub-solves proved optimal. This should be promoted to a headline result, not left as an aside.

5. **[MAJOR] Zero engagement with the LNS / VLSN / matheuristic literature that the paper's own contribution belongs to.** L. 215 asserts *"Large-neighbourhood search with an exactly solved sub-problem is a standard metaheuristic pattern"* with no citation; `refs.bib` contains no LNS/VLSN/matheuristic reference at all. *Remedy:* position Theorem 1 relative to improvement-graph/VLSN theory (Ahuja, Ergun, Orlin & Punnen, DAM 2002) and the exact-subproblem matheuristics (local branching, RINS, corridor method), and state precisely what is new relative to them. Without this a COR referee cannot assess novelty.

6. **[MAJOR] The applications paragraph (l. 96–109) — the paper's entire claim to OR relevance — contains no citations.** Scheduling, wireless interference, map labelling, computer vision, entity resolution are all asserted. *Remedy:* cite each, preferably from OR journals.

7. **[MAJOR] Factual misattribution used to establish OR fit.** L. 119–121: *"Local search methods developed in the operations research literature, notably NuMVC \cite{cai2013numvc} and FastVC \cite{cai2017fastvc}"*. Both are AI-community publications (JAIR 2013; IJCAI 2015 / JAIR 2017). *Remedy:* correct the attribution and replace it with genuine OR local-search references for stable set / clique.

8. **[MAJOR] Three direct text–table contradictions, all downstream of issue 1.** (a) l. 640 names FastVC as the strongest baseline on `del18`/`del20`; FastVC is absent from Table 2. (b) l. 667: *"on `soc-pokec` it is 0.06% behind FastVC, the best value there"*; Table 4 bolds NearLinear's 789161. ## 7. Recommendation

### **Major Revision** — bordering on Reject, and I would not object if the handling editor escalated.

**Justification.** The manuscript contains a real idea (reduction rules applied inside an incumbent-defined, provably separated neighbourhood, making an exact sub-solve an inner-loop operation), competent theory, good engineering, and an experimental protocol whose rigour — independent verification of every returned set, whole-process budget accounting, cross-hardware replication with the build confound measured rather than assumed away, and the BHOSLIB clique-complement warning — is better than most of what I see in this area. It also contains, in §5.12, an honest and accurate statement of how narrow the result is. But three things stand between it and acceptance in COR. First, the empirical result does not support the claim the front matter makes: three wins of 0.03–0.09% on author-generated instances, no test separating it from ReduMIS, an order-of-magnitude time disadvantage on the graphs that kernelize away, and six structural losses on BHOSLIB. Second — and decisively — the two aggregate instruments that carry the paper's case do not hold up under checking: Tables 2–6 and 8 omit the two baselines that beat the method, so the printed mean gap (0.414%), max gap (2.857%) and #best (17) are all wrong in the authors' favour and conceal that FastVC has the best mean gap on the benchmark; and the Wilcoxon test is described twice as being computed over relative gaps when it was computed over raw sizes, where the described method would reduce "ahead of five of six baselines" to three, losing FastVC and NuMVC. Those two defects are fixable and I have no reason to think they are anything but a table-generation bug plus a drafting slip, but until they are fixed the paper's central empirical claims cannot be assessed, and after they are fixed the remaining positive result is too small to carry a SOTA framing. Third, the paper does not engage the OR literature: one COR reference out of 28, no LNS/VLSN/matheuristic citations at all despite claiming to instantiate that pattern, an uncited applications paragraph, and a false attribution of NuMVC/FastVC to the OR literature. My advice to the authors is not to try to strengthen the win but to change what the paper claims: repair the tables and the test, promote the unmeasured region-kernelization mechanism into a measured headline result, convert "where does this help?" from a concession into a designed experiment over kernel fraction, connect the theory to VLSN and matheuristics, and cut the manuscript from 42 to about 28 pages. That paper — a theory-plus-engineering paper about a neighbourhood and its domain of applicability — would be a welcome COR contribution. The present one is not.
