\title{Exact Large-Neighbourhood Search for the Maximum Independent Set
       Problem on Massive Sparse Graphs}

\author[buv]{Quang-Vinh Dang\corref{cor1}}
\ead{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\affiliation[buv]{organization={British University Vietnam},
                  city={Hung Yen}, country={Vietnam}}

\begin{abstract}
Safe reduction rules followed by local search are the standard way to attack the
maximum independence set problem on large sparse graphs. We study a region move:
release a set $F$ of incumbent vertices, collect the outside vertices whose
incumbent neighbours all lie in $F$, and re-solve that region by
branch-and-reduce. Nothing outside the region conflicts with it, so the incumbent
never worsens. The region construction is known from dynamic independent-set
maintenance; we apply it to static graphs, run reductions inside each region and
adapt $|F|$ online. Against six solvers from 2012--2019 on 27 instances at
60\,s, the method is strictly ahead on three Delaunay graphs from one generator
seed, ties on 13 and is behind on 11, and is not significantly different from
ReduMIS, NuMVC or FastVC. Ablations attribute the gain to exact repair and to
reductions inside the region. More recent solvers were not run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Given an undirected graph $G=(V,E)$, the maximum independent set problem asks
for a largest set of pairwise non-adjacent vertices. It is equivalent to the
minimum vertex cover problem on $G$ and to the maximum clique problem on the
complement of $G$. The problem is NP-hard and, unless P${}={}$NP, admits no
constant-factor approximation, so instances of practical size are attacked
heuristically.

The formulation is a natural model wherever a largest mutually compatible
selection is wanted and incompatibility is pairwise. In scheduling, vertices are
tasks and edges record conflicting resource demands, so an independent set is a
largest set of jobs that can run together. In wireless networks, vertices are
transmitters and edges record interference, and an independent set is a largest
simultaneously active subset. In map labelling and in computer vision,
candidate placements or detections that overlap are joined by an edge, and the
largest consistent selection is again an independent set. In entity resolution
and de-duplication over large record collections, edges express that two records
cannot both be kept as distinct entities. What these share is scale and
sparsity: the conflict graphs arising from real data routinely have millions of
vertices and average degree in the low tens, which is the regime this paper
addresses.

Over the last decade the practical state of the art has converged on a single
template: apply \emph{safe data reduction rules} until none fires, then search
whatever survives. The rules are exact, in the sense that they preserve the
optimum, and on many real-world graphs they dissolve the instance outright.
Solvers differ almost entirely in what they do once the rules stall. Reducing-
peeling \citep{chang2017} performs one greedy descent and never revisits a
decision. ReduMIS \citep{lamm2017} and OnlineMIS \citep{dahlum2016} run swap-based
local search on the reduced graph. Branch-and-reduce \citep{akiba2016} explores
the decisions exhaustively and is complete but exponential. Local search
methods developed in the operations research literature, notably NuMVC
\citep{cai2013numvc} and FastVC \citep{cai2017fastvc}, dominate small dense
instances but, as our experiments confirm, either fail outright or lose ground
on graphs with millions of vertices.

All of these share a weakness in the same place: instances whose reduced graph,
or \emph{kernel}, remains large. Delaunay triangulations are the canonical
example, and they are where the published solvers are furthest from each other
and from the optimum.

\paragraph{The idea in one example} Suppose a maximal independent set $S$ is in
hand and nothing can be added. A classical $(1,2)$-swap asks: if I remove one
vertex $v$ of $S$, do two non-adjacent vertices become available? Those two must
have $v$ as their \emph{only} neighbour in $S$. The same question can be asked
about two or ten removed vertices at once: remove a set $F$ of solution vertices
and collect every outside vertex all of whose solution neighbours lie in $F$.
These vertices, together with $F$, form a \emph{region}; nothing outside the
region touches the solution inside it, so any independent set of the region
can be swapped in for $F$ without checking the rest of the graph. The best swap
for this $F$ is therefore the maximum independent set of the region, a smaller
instance of the same problem. Figure~\ref{fig:region} shows a case where
releasing one vertex never helps and releasing two does.

This is large-neighbourhood search \citep{hashimoto2019,katzmann2017} for the
independent set problem, and the construction of the region is not new: it is
the one used by \citet{borowitz2025} in the dynamic setting, where an edge
update triggers an exactly solved local subproblem
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). What this paper contributes is the use of that
construction as the inner move of a \emph{static} solver for massive graphs, the
engineering that makes it cheap enough to run thousands of times per
second, an online rule for how many solution vertices to release, and an
empirical account---including negative results---of where this helps.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a static region-move local search for maximum independent set, with a
      proof that an incumbent is never worsened when sub-solves are
      truncated (Section~\ref{sec:move}), and an explicit
      comparison with the region constructions of
      \citet{borowitz2025}, \citet{hashimoto2019} and swap
      neighbourhoods (Table~\ref{tab:frameworks});
\item an online rule for the number of released vertices, driven by whether
      sub-problems are solved to proven optimality, with a stated schedule
      (Section~\ref{sec:search}) and a sensitivity study;
\item a dynamic graph with last-in-first-out undo that lets reductions run
      inside the move (Section~\ref{sec:implementation});
\item a computational study with every reported solution independently verified
      where the baseline emits one, including measurements that tie the method's
      behaviour to the size of the residual kernel, to whether the region
      sub-solve is exact, and to whether reductions are applied inside the
      region (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). The positive result is narrow
      and we delimit it.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Data reductions} The reduction approach traces back to the
Nemhauser--Trotter theorem \citep{nemhauser1975}, which shows that the vertex
cover linear program is half-integral and that its integral part may be fixed
without losing an optimum. Parameterized complexity supplied the combinatorial
counterpart: vertex cover admits a kernel with at most $2k$ vertices, obtained
either from that linear program or from crown decompositions
\citep{chen2001,abukhzam2007}. In parallel, the line of exact exponential
algorithms that began with \citet{tarjan1977} and was
sharpened by measure-and-conquer analysis \citep{fomin2009} produced most of the
local rules now used in practice, including the confining-set machinery behind
the unconfined rule \citep{xiao2013,xiao2017}.

\citet{akiba2016} first demonstrated that a branch-and-reduce
algorithm carrying the full rule set is competitive with, and often superior to,
integer programming on real instances, and established the rule ordering that
subsequent solvers reuse. \citet{hespe2019} parallelised
kernelization, introduced dependency checking to avoid re-testing rules that
cannot fire, and characterised which instance families reduce well; their
measurements are the source of the observation that success is essentially
determined by whether the kernel becomes small. \citet{gellner2021} showed that transformations which temporarily \emph{increase}
the graph can unlock further reduction, and \citet{figiel2022}
that \emph{undoing} a rule can enable others, yielding a smaller final kernel.
Branching itself can be aimed at provoking reductions \citep{hespe2021}, and
reduction-based solvers have been combined with SAT solving
\citep{plachetta2021}. The weighted variant has attracted most of the recent
activity \citep{lamm2019,xiaowww2021,grossmann2023,langedal2022}.

\paragraph{Local search} \citet{andrade2012} introduced the
$(1,2)$-swap neighbourhood with an $O(m)$ test for improving moves, which
underpins the KaMIS solvers \citep{lamm2017,dahlum2016}. In the operations
research and artificial intelligence literature the same problem is usually
posed as minimum vertex cover. NuMVC \citep{cai2013numvc} combines edge weighting
with a two-stage exchange step and configuration checking with forgetting, and
is extremely strong on the dense structured instances of the DIMACS and BHOSLIB
collections. Its cost per step, however, makes it unsuitable for massive graphs,
which motivated FastVC \citep{cai2015,cai2017fastvc}: a cheaper construction
together with best-from-multiple-selection, trading some quality for the ability
to run on such graphs at all. Our experiments reproduce both halves of this
picture, including instances on which NuMVC returns no solution.

\paragraph{Exact solvers} Branch-and-bound on the complement with incremental
MaxSAT reasoning \citep{li2017momc} is the strongest general approach on dense
instances. The solver that won the vertex cover track of the PACE 2019
challenge \citep{pace2019} is a portfolio combining aggressive kernelization,
iterated local search, branch-and-reduce and two calls to such a clique solver
\citep{hespe2020}; it is among our baselines, and remains far stronger than our
own exact mode on dense instances for exactly this reason.

\paragraph{Neighbourhood search with exactly solved subproblems}
\citet{andrade2012} introduced the $(1,2)$-swap and, more generally, $(j,k)$-swaps
for the unweighted problem; \citet{hashimoto2019} extend the $(k,\ell)$
neighbourhood to the weighted problem and embed a branch-and-bound solver in the
local search so that a very large neighbourhood can be explored.
\citet{katzmann2017} search the $k$-swap neighbourhood of minimum vertex cover
systematically, with a running time of $\Delta^{O(k)}n$ for maximum degree
$\Delta$, and report that larger $k$ pays off. The closest work is that of
\citet{borowitz2025}, who maintain an independent set under edge updates. After
an update that the greedy repair cannot absorb, they build a subgraph $H$ by a
breadth-first search of bounded depth $d$ around the endpoints, add the incumbent
neighbours of $H$ so that no incumbent vertex outside $H$ is adjacent to it, add
the ``tight'' vertices of $N(H)\setminus H$, and solve the induced subgraph with
the branch-and-reduce solver of KaMIS \citep{lamm2019}, which applies the full
reduction suite exhaustively. The size of $H$ is capped by a parameter
$\nu_{\max}$, and high-degree vertices are pruned from $H$ before solving.

The region of Section~\ref{sec:move} is the same object: if $F$ is the set of
incumbent vertices in their subgraph after closing it, their region is $R(F)$.
\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:region} is therefore not a new observation}; it is the
statement that justifies their construction, and we state and prove it for
self-containedness. The \emph{use of reductions on the region} is likewise
present in their work, through the sub-solver. Table~\ref{tab:frameworks}
lists what differs: the setting (static against dynamic), how the released set
is chosen (counting incumbent vertices along a breadth-first walk, against
a depth limit on all vertices), how its size is controlled (an online rule
against a fixed cap), the pruning step (absent here, so every sub-solve is
exact whenever it completes), and the sub-solver (an in-house reusable
branch-and-reduce engine with $O(\deg)$ undo, whose reuse across moves is what
sets the move rate). Whether those differences matter on static graphs is an
empirical question, and Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures the parts we can
isolate within our own implementation: exact against heuristic repair, and
reductions inside the region against none. We did not run their code.

\paragraph{Recent local search for vertex cover} Local search for minimum vertex
cover on massive graphs has continued to develop since FastVC. InfVC
\citep{sun2025infvc} adds inference rules to the search and a degree-biased
perturbation, and reports gains over earlier solvers on massive instances. We
did not run it; see Section~\ref{sec:threats}. For the weighted problem,
\citet{grossmann2024survey} survey the reduction rules developed to date, and
\citet{grossmann2025chils} combine learned screening of reductions with a
concurrent iterated local search that reports gains on the largest standard
instances. These are weighted solvers and were not run here.

\section{Preliminaries}
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a simple undirected graph. A set $S \subseteq V$ is
\emph{independent} if no edge of $G$ has both endpoints in $S$; $\alpha(G)$
denotes the maximum size of such a set. For $v \in V$ we write $N(v)$ for its
neighbourhood and $N[v] = N(v) \cup \{v\}$.

\begin{definition}[Safe reduction]
A rule transforming $G$ into $G'$ with an offset $c$ is \emph{safe} if
$\alpha(G) = \alpha(G') + c$ and a maximum independent set of $G$ can be
reconstructed from one of $G'$.
\end{definition}

We use the rule set that is standard in this literature
\citep{akiba2016,lamm2017,hespe2019}, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:rules}. A
graph on which no rule fires is called a \emph{kernel}. Each rule records how to
reconstruct a solution, and \emph{lifting} replays those records in reverse; for
instance a fold of $v$ with non-adjacent neighbours $u,w$ into a new vertex $f$
records that $u$ and $w$ are taken when $f$ ends up in the solution and $v$
otherwise.

Three entries of Table~\ref{tab:rules} are less self-explanatory than the rest.

\paragraph{Twin} Two non-adjacent vertices $u,v$ of degree three with the same
neighbourhood $\{p,q,r\}$ are twins. Any solution that uses one of $p,q,r$ gives
up both $u$ and $v$, so only two outcomes matter: both twins are taken, or all
of $p,q,r$ are. If the neighbourhood contains an edge, say $pq$, at most two of
$p,q,r$ can be taken, so taking $u$ and $v$ is never worse and we do that
(delete $p,q,r$). If it is independent, neither outcome dominates, and we
\emph{fold}: replace the five vertices by a single vertex $f$ adjacent to every
other neighbour of $p,q,r$ and add $2$ to the offset, so that $f$ in the reduced
solution stands for $\{p,q,r\}$ and $f$ absent stands for $\{u,v\}$
\citep{akiba2016}. In the first case the argument is a one-line exchange:
if an optimal $S$ meets $\{p,q,r\}$ in $T \neq \emptyset$ then $u,v \notin S$ and
$|T|\le 2$, so $(S\setminus T)\cup\{u,v\}$ is independent and no smaller; if
$T=\emptyset$ then maximality puts $u,v$ in $S$.

\paragraph{Unconfined} Start from $S=\{v\}$. A vertex $u \in N(S)$ with exactly
one neighbour in $S$ is a \emph{child} of $S$. If some child $u$ has
$N(u)\subseteq N[S]$, then swapping $S$ for $u$ in any independent set that
contains $v$ cannot lose anything, so some optimum avoids $v$ and $v$ is
discarded. If the child's remaining neighbourhood $N(u)\setminus N[S]$ is a
single vertex $w$, then $w$ is added to $S$ and the test continues; otherwise
the test stops and $v$ is \emph{confined}. The rule generalises domination, which
is the case $S=\{v\}$ with $N[u]\subseteq N[v]$ \citep{xiao2013,xiao2017}. We
cap $|S|$ at eight and the edges scanned per test at $2\times 10^4$; a capped
test simply reports ``confined'', so the cap makes the rule fire less often but
never unsound.

\paragraph{LP} Relax the vertex cover problem to $x_v\in[0,1]$ with
$x_u+x_v\ge 1$ on every edge. Its optimum is half-integral
\citep{nemhauser1975}, and any optimal half-integral $x$ has a maximum
independent set that contains every vertex with $x_v=0$ and avoids every vertex
with $x_v=1$; only vertices at $\tfrac12$ remain. The optimum is read from a
maximum matching in the bipartite double cover via K\"onig's theorem. This is the only
rule that looks at the whole graph rather than a neighbourhood, which is why it
fires where the local rules have stalled. Because it must run to completion for
persistency to hold, it is skipped rather than interrupted when the budget is
short.

For completeness we record why the two rules that are least obvious are safe.

\begin{lemma}[Simplicial vertices]
\label{lem:simplicial}
If $N(v)$ induces a clique then some maximum independent set contains $v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be a maximum independent set with $v \notin S$. Since $N(v)$ is a
clique, $|S \cap N(v)| \le 1$. If $S \cap N(v) = \emptyset$ then $S \cup \{v\}$
is independent and larger, contradicting maximality. Otherwise
$S \cap N(v) = \{u\}$ for a single $u$, and $(S \setminus \{u\}) \cup \{v\}$ is
independent, of the same size, and contains $v$.
\end{proof}

Degree-0 and degree-1 vertices are the special cases $|N(v)| \in \{0,1\}$.

\begin{lemma}[Domination]
\label{lem:domination}
If $uv \in E$ and $N[u] \subseteq N[v]$ then some maximum independent set
excludes $v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be a maximum independent set containing $v$. Then $u \notin S$, because
$u$ and $v$ are adjacent. Consider $S' = (S \setminus \{v\}) \cup \{u\}$. Any
neighbour of $u$ lies in $N[u] \subseteq N[v]$, that is, it is either $v$, which
has been removed, or a neighbour of $v$, which is not in $S$ because $v \in S$.
Hence $S'$ is independent, has the same size as $S$, and excludes $v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Degree-2 folding]
\label{lem:fold}
Let $N(v) = \{u,w\}$ with $uw \notin E$, and let $G'$ be obtained from $G$ by
deleting $v,u,w$ and adding a vertex $f$ adjacent to
$(N(u) \cup N(w)) \setminus \{v,u,w\}$. Then $\alpha(G) = \alpha(G') + 1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
($\ge$) Let $T$ be a maximum independent set of $G'$. If $f \notin T$ then
$T \cup \{v\}$ is independent in $G$, since $v$'s only neighbours are $u$ and
$w$, neither of which is in $T$. If $f \in T$ then $(T \setminus \{f\}) \cup
\{u,w\}$ is independent in $G$: $u$ and $w$ are non-adjacent by assumption, and
any other neighbour of $u$ or $w$ is adjacent to $f$ and hence outside $T$.
Either way we obtain an independent set of size $|T| + 1$.

($\le$) Let $S$ be a maximum independent set of $G$. We first argue that $S$
may be taken to contain $v$, or to contain both $u$ and $w$. Note that
Lemma~\ref{lem:simplicial} is \emph{not} available here: its hypothesis is that
$N(v)$ induces a clique, and $N(v) = \{u,w\}$ with $uw \notin E$ is precisely the
case in which it does not. We argue directly instead. If $S$ contains neither
$u$ nor $w$, then $S \cup \{v\}$ is independent, because $u$ and $w$ are the only
neighbours of $v$; maximality of $S$ therefore forces $v \in S$ already. If $S$
contains exactly one of them, say $u$, then $(S \setminus \{u\}) \cup \{v\}$ is
independent --- the only neighbours of $v$ are $u$, which has been removed, and
$w$, which is not in $S$ --- and has the same size, so we may replace $S$ by it
and again have $v \in S$. In the one remaining case $u,w \in S$, and then
$v \notin S$ because $v$ is adjacent to both. In the first case
$S \setminus \{v\}$ is independent in $G'$ and avoids $f$. In the second,
$(S \setminus \{u,w\}) \cup \{f\}$ is independent in $G'$, because no neighbour
of $f$ other than through $u$ or $w$ exists. Either way we obtain an independent
set of $G'$ of size $|S| - 1$.
\end{proof}

\section{The region move}
\label{sec:move}

Let $S$ be an independent set of $G$ and let $F \subseteq S$. Define
\begin{equation}
R(F) \;=\; F \;\cup\; \{\, u \in V \setminus S \;:\; N(u) \cap S \subseteq F \,\}.
\label{eq:region}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:region}
With $S$, $F$ and $R(F)$ as above:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] no edge of $G$ joins $R(F)$ to $S \setminus R(F)$;
\item[(b)] for every independent set $T$ of the induced subgraph $G[R(F)]$, the
      set $(S \setminus R(F)) \cup T$ is independent in $G$;
\item[(c)] $S \cap R(F) = F$, and consequently $\alpha(G[R(F)]) \ge |F|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $v \in R(F)$ and $w \in S \setminus R(F)$ and suppose $vw \in E$. If
$v \in F$ then $v,w \in S$ are adjacent, contradicting independence of $S$.
Otherwise $v \notin S$, so $w \in N(v) \cap S \subseteq F \subseteq R(F)$,
contradicting $w \notin R(F)$.

(b) $T$ is independent, $S \setminus R(F)$ is independent as a subset of $S$,
and by (a) no edge joins the two sets.

(c) $F \subseteq S \cap R(F)$ holds by construction. Conversely, an
$x \in S \cap R(F)$ with $x \notin F$ would belong to $V \setminus S$ by
\eqref{eq:region}, a contradiction. Hence $S \cap R(F) = F$, which is an
independent set of $G[R(F)]$, so $\alpha(G[R(F)]) \ge |F|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Attribution]
\label{rem:attr}
Part~(a) is the closure property that \citet{borowitz2025} obtain by adding the
incumbent neighbours of $H$ and the tight vertices of $N(H)\setminus H$; their
subgraph is $R(F)$ for $F$ the incumbent vertices it contains. We claim
the statements and proofs below only as a self-contained account of why the move
is admissible, not as a new construction.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Residual form]
\label{prop:residual}
$R(F) = V \setminus N[S \setminus F]$. Hence solving $G[R(F)]$ is exactly the
maximum independent set problem on the residual graph obtained by fixing
$S \setminus F$ into the solution.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A vertex $u \notin S$ lies in $R(F)$ iff $N(u)\cap S\subseteq F$ iff $u$ has no
neighbour in $S\setminus F$ iff $u \notin N[S\setminus F]$ (it is not in
$S \setminus F$ itself). A vertex $u\in S$ lies in $R(F)$ iff $u\in F$ iff
$u\notin S\setminus F$; and every vertex of $S \setminus F$ is in
$N[S\setminus F]$, while a vertex of $F$ is not adjacent to $S\setminus F$
because $S$ is independent.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:residual} is the framing under which the move is a
standard ``fix and optimise'' large-neighbourhood step: choose which part of the
incumbent to keep, and re-solve the rest. It also explains why the region cannot
be enlarged by hand: any vertex outside $R(F)$ is adjacent to a retained
vertex. What distinguishes variants of the idea is how $F$ is chosen, how large
it is allowed to become, and how the residual problem is solved
(Table~\ref{tab:frameworks}).

\begin{corollary}[Monotonicity]
\label{cor:monotone}
Let $T^\star$ be a maximum independent set of $G[R(F)]$. Then
$S' = (S \setminus R(F)) \cup T^\star$ is independent and
$|S'| = |S| - |F| + |T^\star| \ge |S|$.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:monotone} assumes the subproblem is solved to optimality,
which Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade} cannot guarantee: its sub-solver runs under a
time bound and may return a set that is merely independent. Two further
statements are therefore needed before the guarantee transfers to the
implemented move.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded sub-solves]
\label{prop:bounded}
Let $T$ be any independent set of $G[R(F)]$, not necessarily maximum, and let
$S' = (S \setminus R(F)) \cup T$. Then $S'$ is independent, and
$|S'| \ge |S|$ if and only if $|T| \ge |F|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Independence is Theorem~\ref{thm:region}(b), whose proof assumes nothing about
$T$ beyond independence in $G[R(F)]$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:region}(c) we have
$S \cap R(F) = F$, so removing $R(F)$ from $S$ deletes exactly $|F|$ vertices and
$|S'| = |S| - |F| + |T|$; the equivalence follows.
\end{proof}

The test $|T| \ge |F|$ is what the implementation applies before a region is
written back, and it is what keeps the move monotone when the sub-solve is
truncated; Corollary~\ref{cor:monotone} is the special case in which the
sub-solve completes, since then $|T^\star| \ge |F|$ holds automatically by
Theorem~\ref{thm:region}(c). Monotonicity thus does not depend on the sub-solver
proving optimality, only on a smaller region solution never being accepted.

\begin{proposition}[Maximality]
\label{prop:maximal}
If $S$ is maximal in $G$ and $T$ is maximal in $G[R(F)]$, then
$S' = (S \setminus R(F)) \cup T$ is maximal in $G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $u \notin S'$. If $u \in R(F)$ then $u \notin T$, and maximality of $T$ in
$G[R(F)]$ gives $u$ a neighbour in $T \subseteq S'$. If $u \notin R(F)$ then
$u \notin S$, for otherwise $u \in S \setminus R(F) \subseteq S'$. Since
$u \notin R(F)$ and $u \notin S$, definition \eqref{eq:region} gives
$N(u) \cap S \not\subseteq F$, so some $w \in N(u) \cap S$ has $w \notin F$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:region}(c), $w \notin R(F)$, hence
$w \in S \setminus R(F) \subseteq S'$. Either way $u$ has a neighbour in $S'$.
\end{proof}

Maximality of $S'$ therefore also rests on the sub-solve completing. A truncated
solve can return a $T$ that is independent but not maximal, and then a vertex of
$R(F) \setminus T$ may be left with no neighbour in $S'$ at all, because the
incumbent neighbours that blocked it all lay in $F$ and were released. The
implementation repairs this by scanning the vertices adjacent to the region and
taking any that have become free, which only adds vertices and so preserves
Proposition~\ref{prop:bounded}. Proposition~\ref{prop:maximal} is the statement
that no such repair is needed anywhere else: vertices outside $R(F)$ retain a
blocking neighbour by construction, so the scan is confined to the region and its
neighbourhood rather than to the graph.

The region is cheap to construct if the number of incumbent neighbours is
maintained.

\begin{proposition}[Cost of a move]
\label{prop:cost}
Let $t(u) = |N(u) \cap S|$ be maintained incrementally. Then $R(F)$ can be
constructed in time $O\!\left(\sum_{v \in F} \deg v\right)$, and the induced
subgraph $G[R(F)]$ in time $O\!\left(\sum_{v \in R(F)} \deg v\right)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Scanning the neighbourhoods of the vertices of $F$ and counting, for each
vertex $u$ outside $S$, how many of its neighbours lie in $F$, costs
$\sum_{v \in F} \deg v$. A vertex $u$ satisfies $N(u) \cap S \subseteq F$
exactly when that count equals $t(u)$, so membership in $R(F)$ is decided in
constant time per candidate, and only vertices adjacent to $F$ are candidates.
Maintaining $t$ costs $O(\deg v)$ whenever a vertex $v$ enters or leaves $S$,
which is charged to the move that changes it.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:cost} bounds the construction of the region from a set $F$
that is already given; selecting $F$ is a separate cost that it does not cover.
The implementation grows $F$ by a breadth-first walk from a seed vertex,
collecting incumbent vertices until the target size is reached and capping the
number of vertices visited. The walk scans the full adjacency of each vertex it
visits, so its cost is the total degree of the ball rather than of $F$, and a
single step that reaches a high-degree vertex dominates the move: the largest
degree in our benchmark is $100{,}029$. The per-move cost is therefore bounded by
the total degree of the visited ball together with that of the region, and not by
$|F|$.

Subject to that, the cost of the move is dominated not by the size of the graph
but by the total degree of the region, and the exact sub-solve is performed on the
kernel of $G[R(F)]$ rather than on $G[R(F)]$ itself.

\begin{corollary}[Relation to classical swaps]
\label{cor:swaps}
For $F = \{v\}$, $R(F) = \{v\} \cup \{u \notin S : N(u) \cap S = \{v\}\}$, which
is the set examined by a $(1,2)$-swap \citep{andrade2012}. Solving $G[R(F)]$
therefore finds the best $(1,k)$-swap at $v$ for every $k$ at once.
\end{corollary}

More generally, a region move that releases $|F| = j$ vertices and writes back an
independent set of size $k$ removes $j$ solution vertices and inserts $k$, so it
\emph{is} a $(j,k)$-swap. The neighbourhood is thus not a new class of move:
$(j,k)$-swaps are the generalisation of the classical two-improvement introduced
by \citet{andrade2012}, and the present construction should not be
read as reaching outside that class. What Theorem~\ref{thm:region} supplies is
not a larger class but a way to search it: because $R(F)$ is separated from
$S \setminus R(F)$, the best $(j,k)$-swap at $F$ over all $k$ is obtained by
solving one induced subproblem, with no consistency check against the rest of the
incumbent. That is what makes $j$ in the tens affordable, where implementations
that enumerate swaps directly are in practice confined to $j \le 2$.

\section{Algorithm and implementation}
\label{sec:algorithm}

\subsection{The search}\label{sec:search}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the shape of the method and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade} gives the overall structure. Kernelization runs
once; a \emph{dive} then constructs an initial solution by repeatedly discarding
a maximum-degree vertex and re-reducing, which reproduces reducing-peeling
\citep{chang2017}; the remaining budget goes to region moves.

\centering

stopped improving, and the escape mechanisms exist to put it back.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{\textsc{Cascade}: static region-move search}
\label{alg:cascade}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require graph $G=(V,E)$, wall-clock budget $B$, seed; parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params}
\Ensure a maximal independent set $S^\star$ of $G$ (best found within $B$)
\State $(K,\mathit{trail}) \gets \textsc{Reduce}(G)$ \Comment{full rules; stops after $\kappa B$}
\State dive on $K$: discard a maximum-degree vertex, re-reduce (cheap rules), until $K=\emptyset$
\State $S \gets \textsc{Maximalise}(\textsc{Lift}(\mathit{trail}))$;\; $S^\star \gets S$
\State $W \gets$ shuffled kernel vertices and their neighbours;\; $k \gets k_0$;\; $\mathit{plateau}\gets\textbf{false}$
\While{time remains}
  \Repeat \Comment{one batch of $64$ region moves}
    \If{$W$ is empty} \Comment{a sweep has finished}
      \If{the sweep gained nothing} $\mathit{plateau}\gets\textbf{true}$;\; $k \gets k + \lfloor k/4\rfloor + 1$ \textbf{else} $\mathit{plateau}\gets\textbf{false}$
      \EndIf
      \State refill $W$ with all seeds, shuffled
    \EndIf
    \State $v \gets W.\textsc{pop}()$
    \State $F \gets$ first $k$ incumbent vertices on a BFS walk from $v$ \Comment{$\le\max(64,64k)$ visited}
    \State $R \gets F \cup \{u \notin S : N(u) \cap S \subseteq F\}$;\; build $G[R]$
    \State $T \gets \textsc{BranchAndReduce}(G[R])$ within node budget $\nu$, time slice $\sigma$
    \If{$|T| > |F|$ \textbf{or} ($|T|=|F|$, $T\neq F$ \textbf{and} $\mathit{plateau}$)}
      \State $S \gets (S \setminus R) \cup T$;\; add any vertex left with no neighbour in $S$; push $R\cup N(R)$ onto $W$
    \EndIf
    \If{$T$ not proved optimal \textbf{or} $|R|>\rho$} $k \gets \max(1,\lfloor k/2\rfloor)$;\; re-queue $v$
    \ElsIf{no gain} \textbf{if} $s$ consecutive proved moves without gain \textbf{then} $k \gets \min(k_{\max},2k)$
    \EndIf
  \Until{64 moves done}
  \State $S^\star \gets \max(S^\star, S)$
  \State \textsc{Perturb}$(S)$: force $q$ random kernel-side vertices in, evict their neighbours, run $24$ region moves; keep if not worse, else undo
  \If{$q$ at its maximum and $r$ consecutive kicks failed \textbf{or} no improvement for $\tau\times$(first-dive time)}
    \State \textbf{restart}: $S \gets$ fresh randomised dive; keep $S^\star$
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return $S^\star$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The inner loop therefore has two clocks. \emph{Within} a sweep, $k$ halves when
a region is too large or its sub-solve is cut off, and doubles when $s=512$
consecutive regions were solved to proven optimality without a gain. \emph{Between}
sweeps, a sweep with no gain at all raises $k$ by a quarter, switches on
plateau moves, and enlarges the sub-solve budgets. Plateau moves accept a region
optimum that equals the released set in size but differs from it; randomised
tie-breaking in the sub-solver makes repeated solves of one region return
different optima. A perturbation runs after every batch of 64 region moves.

\paragraph{The decision-space move} The dive leaves behind a stack of decisions,
one per discarded vertex. An alternative move operates on that stack rather than
on the graph: it rewinds to a chosen level, reverses the decision taken there and
re-dives from that point, so the cost is the suffix of the dive rather than the
whole instance. This was the first move we designed, and it is the
\textsc{dive} variant ablated in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}; it survives in the
implementation as an option but not as a default, for the reasons reported there.
It is described here only so that the ablation has something to refer to --- it
has no separation property, and Theorem~\ref{thm:region} says nothing about it.

\paragraph{Where the search looks} Every vertex decided by a reduction belongs
to some maximum independent set, so an improving move must touch a vertex that
survived into the kernel. Seeding the sweep from the kernel and its neighbours
rather than from all of $V$ matters on graphs that reduce well: a graph such as
\texttt{com-lj} has four million vertices and reduces to $1781$ at a fixed point
(Table~\ref{tab:kernels}).

\paragraph{Choosing the neighbourhood size} The useful value of $k=|F|$ differs
by orders of magnitude across instance families, so it is inferred rather than
set; the rule is the one in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade} and the text below it.
On meshes it settles at tens of released vertices; on dense instances at one or
two, where the move degenerates to a classical swap. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}
varies the starting value $k_0$ over three orders of magnitude.

\paragraph{Escaping local optima} Because region moves never degrade the
incumbent, the search stalls at a solution that is optimal for every region of
the current size. Three mechanisms address this. \emph{Plateau moves} accept a
region optimum of equal size that differs from the incumbent, once a full sweep
has stopped producing gains; randomised tie-breaking in the sub-solver makes
repeated solves of the same region return different optima. \emph{Perturbation}
forces a vertex into the solution, evicts its blocking neighbours and lets
region moves repair the damage, with a change log that rolls back only the
touched vertices if the result is worse. \emph{Restarts} discard the incumbent
and dive again, keeping the best solution in an archive; the trigger is idle
time measured in multiples of the cost of one dive, since a restart must pay for
a re-dive. This keying matters: on the smallest instances the algorithm restarts
hundreds of times within a minute, while on the largest it never restarts.

\subsection{Data structures and throughput}
\label{sec:implementation}

Reductions and branching decisions both mutate the graph and must be reversible,
at every node of the sub-searches and after every region move. We use a compressed
adjacency array in which each entry stores the position of its twin, together
with a per-vertex pointer separating the live neighbours from the dead ones.
Deleting a vertex $v$ swaps its entry to the end of each live neighbour's range
and decrements that neighbour's pointer; undoing the deletion merely increments
the pointers again. Nothing has to be swapped back, because no later operation
touches an entry outside the live range. Both directions cost $O(\deg v)$ and
carry no payload, and degrees remain exact throughout.

Degree-2 folding introduces edges that do not fit in the static array; these go
into per-vertex overflow lists which are append-only and undone by a pop, and
which are allocated only for vertices that actually take part in a fold.

Two engineering details matter for throughput. First, each node of a sub-search
owns an exact list of its live vertices, built by filtering its parent's. An
earlier version shared one list and could drop a vertex when a fold created a new
one; the clique-cover bound then failed to cover that vertex, came out too small,
and pruned optimal solutions while still reporting that optimality had been
proven. Second, the sub-solver is reused across region moves instead of being
constructed afresh, since at a hundred thousand moves per minute the set-up of
the graph and reduction state costs more than the search itself.

To give these choices a number, the full configuration in the ablation runs of
Section~\ref{sec:regional} (three seeds, three processes sharing the machine)
kernelizes \texttt{del18}, 262\,144 vertices, in $1.6$--$2.0$\,s, completes the
first dive in a further $0.55$--$0.63$\,s and performs $102\,906$--$112\,414$
region moves in the rest of the 60\,s, one every $560\,\mu$s on average. On
\texttt{web-Stanford} the figures are $1.0$--$1.1$\,s, $0.03$--$0.05$\,s and
$70\,496$--$91\,130$ moves. Those rates are what the reuse of the sub-solver and
the payload-free undo buy; the throughput, not the asymptotics, is what makes
an exactly-solved neighbourhood practical.

The reduction queue is seeded in increasing order of degree, so that the cheap
rules fire on the vertices most likely to trigger cascades before any expensive
test is attempted on a hub. This is a design choice, not a measured result of
this paper: we did not keep a plain-order variant and report no comparison.

The last of these details is the one that decides whether the solver keeps its
promise at all. Kernelization is run to exhaustion in principle, but on some
graphs exhaustion is unaffordable: on \texttt{soc-pokec}, 1.6 million vertices,
running the rules to a fixed point takes $180.5$\,s in an early measurement on
the main-table machine and $332$\,s in the loaded measurement of
Table~\ref{tab:kernels}, three or more times the entire 60-second budget, so a
solver that insisted on finishing would return nothing at all. We therefore give kernelization a fixed share of the budget --- $0.3$ by
default --- and stop it where it stands when that share is spent, keeping
whatever reductions have been applied and continuing with the rest. On
\texttt{soc-pokec} the phase is cut off at $18.0$\,s, exactly the share, and the
solver returns 788\,868 within its budget. The truncation is recorded in the
run records, so a reader can see on which instances the kernel is partial.
This is the difference between a method that respects a deadline and one that
merely has a time limit in its interface.

Correctness is checked by comparing the exact solver against exhaustive search on
random graphs, repeating the comparison with each reduction rule disabled in
turn, and by verifying that every solution our solver reports is independent and maximal.

\section{Computational study}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:setup-machine}

All runs are performed on the same machine and are single-threaded: an Intel
Xeon at 2.10\,GHz with four cores and 15\,GB of memory, running Ubuntu 24.04,
with every solver compiled by GCC 13.3 at \texttt{-O3}. Every solver
receives the same wall-clock budget, measured around the entire process, so that
reading the instance and any construction phase are charged to the solver that
incurs them. This is deliberate: a solver whose construction phase cannot be
interrupted is genuinely less useful under a deadline, and
Table~\ref{tab:budget} reports how closely each solver keeps to the budget.

Every value is checked independently where the solver emits its set. A separate
program re-reads the original instance, confirms that the returned set contains
no edge, and reports its size; a value that fails this check is not counted. The
exception is the reference implementations of reducing-peeling (NearLinear and
LinearTime), which print a size without the corresponding set. Their values are
included in the tables, marked unverified in the raw records, and taken on trust;
all 54 of their runs are in this category, and one run each of ReduMIS and NuMVC
(the instance on which they returned nothing) is also unverified.

Baselines are built from their authors' published sources, at the revision the
provisioning script pins, and with the authors' own build settings. ReduMIS and
OnlineMIS come from KaMIS \citep{lamm2017,dahlum2016}; reducing-peeling and the
near-linear kernelization from the implementation released with
\citet{chang2017}; NuMVC \citep{cai2013numvc} and FastVC
\citep{cai2017fastvc} from a library that carries the original implementations.
The last two solve the minimum vertex cover formulation, and we convert their
output to an independent set before verification. Each solver is given the
instance in the format its own implementation expects, converted by a single
tool distributed with our code, so that no solver pays a parsing penalty another
avoids.

The main tables carry no exact or integer-programming baseline. Those methods
answer a different question, and on most of this benchmark they do not answer it
within the budget; we report them separately in Section~\ref{sec:exact}, where
the comparison is on their own terms.

\subsection{Instances}

The benchmark comprises 27 instances in five families: Delaunay triangulations
and random geometric graphs, road networks, social networks, web graphs, and the
BHOSLIB \texttt{frb} collection generated from Model RB \citep{xu2007}. The geometric
family is generated by a script included with the source; the rest are public
(SNAP and Network Repository; Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}), and SHA-256
checksums of the downloaded files are distributed with the source.

One instance-preparation issue is worth recording, because it silently produces
wrong answers. The BHOSLIB instances are distributed in \emph{clique} form, and
the copies in common repositories are the dense clique graphs: \texttt{frb30-15-1}
there has 450 vertices and 83,198 edges. A maximum independent set of that file
has size 15, the domain size of the underlying construction, and every solver we
tested returns exactly 15 on it. The instance relevant to the present problem is
the complement, with 17,827 edges and independence number 30, and that is what
we use. Benchmarking the distributed file directly would have every solver agree
on a confidently wrong value.

rules are far more effective, and \textsc{Cascade} and ReduMIS reach the same

a handful of vertices, because the kernels are a small fraction of the input:
\texttt{com-lj} has four million vertices and kernelization run to a fixed point
leaves 1781 of them (Table~\ref{tab:kernels}). What separates the solvers there is robustness rather than
quality, and the differences that remain run in both directions. \textsc{Cascade}
ties ReduMIS exactly on \texttt{com-lj} and is four vertices behind it on
\texttt{as-skitter} out of $1.17$ million; on \texttt{soc-pokec} it is $0.06\%$
behind FastVC, the best value there. Robustness is where the gap is visible:
ReduMIS returned nothing on \texttt{soc-pokec} within eleven minutes, and NuMVC
returned nothing on \texttt{com-lj} within sixteen, while \textsc{Cascade}
produced a verified solution on every instance in the benchmark.

On web graphs \textsc{Cascade} is behind ReduMIS by 15 and 102 vertices, which
is $0.009\%$ and $0.025\%$. On the BHOSLIB family it is the weaker tool. It
reaches the known optimum of \texttt{frb30-15-1}, as do ReduMIS, NuMVC and
FastVC, but on the other six instances it is one or two vertices short of the
best value, and that best value is NuMVC's or FastVC's on every one of them ---
45 against our 43 on \texttt{frb45-21-1}, 58 against our 56 on
\texttt{frb59-26-1}. Because these optima are small, a two-vertex shortfall is a
$4.4\%$ relative gap, the largest we record anywhere. The structural
reason is given in Section~\ref{sec:move}: on a dense graph almost every vertex
outside the incumbent has several incumbent neighbours, so releasing a small
$F$ unlocks nothing while releasing a large one makes the region the whole
graph. The move degenerates, and specialised local search is the better tool.

performance profile, and the Wilcoxon tests. (iii)~\emph{ReduMIS alone}
(Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}, Table~\ref{tab:whence}): 26 instances, because ReduMIS
returned no solution on \texttt{soc-pokec}; there \textsc{Cascade} is ahead on 3,
level on 14 and behind on 9. An earlier version of this study built the quality
tables from five of the seven solvers; the tables, the aggregate and the budget
tables here include all seven.

Over the seven solvers the mean relative gap of \textsc{Cascade} is $0.785\%$,
against $0.340\%$ for FastVC, $0.501\%$ for ReduMIS and $1.219\%$ for NuMVC
(Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}); by that statistic \textsc{Cascade} is third of
seven, because its worst instances are the dense BHOSLIB ones, where one or two
vertices is a gap of several percent. The Wilcoxon tests are run on these
relative gaps and Holm-corrected over the six comparisons. They find
\textsc{Cascade} significantly better than OnlineMIS, NearLinear and LinearTime,
and \emph{no significant difference} from ReduMIS ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.38$),
NuMVC ($0.38$) or FastVC ($0.44$). Table~\ref{tab:whence} decomposes the ReduMIS
comparison by family. Summed over the 26 instances both solved, \textsc{Cascade}
is ahead by 8778 vertices and behind by 127; nearly all the surplus is one
instance, \texttt{del20}, and the deficit is concentrated in the two web graphs.
The reading is that the strong solvers are equal on most of this benchmark,
that \textsc{Cascade} degrades more gracefully on the largest geometric
instance, and that ReduMIS holds a small edge on web graphs.

\emph{Verification coverage.} Two of the three baselines that \textsc{Cascade}
beats significantly, NearLinear and LinearTime, are reducing-peeling programs
that print a size without writing the set, so none of their 54 runs is
verified; NuMVC and ReduMIS each have one unverified run (the instance on which
they returned nothing). All other values are re-derived from an emitted solution.

Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows the same data as a performance profile.
\textsc{Cascade} matches the best value on 16 of the 27 instances, reaches
within $0.07\%$ of it on a further five, and returns a solution on every
instance, which ReduMIS and NuMVC do not. Its curve is then flat from
$\tau \approx 1.0006$ to $\tau \approx 1.026$, and the six instances beyond that
break are the whole of the rest of the BHOSLIB family. The break is an artefact
of scale rather than of behaviour: a one-vertex shortfall on a 35-vertex optimum
is a $2.9\%$ ratio, and a two-vertex shortfall on a 45-vertex optimum is
$4.7\%$, so instances whose absolute loss is one or two vertices sit further to
the right than instances whose absolute loss is a hundred. This is the
complementary weakness of the profile to the one noted for the absolute margins,
and the two views should be read together.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} should be read alongside these results. NuMVC checks its
cutoff only between search steps, so under a 60\,s budget it takes 204\,s on
average and exceeds 90\,s on twelve of the 27 instances; it is therefore
compared here under conditions that favour it, and is still behind.

\subsection{Residual kernels and direct exact solving}
\label{sec:kernels}

The motivation for region moves is a large residual kernel, so the first
measurement is how large the kernel actually is on this benchmark, how long it
takes to reach, and whether the kernel could simply be solved exactly.
Table~\ref{tab:kernels} reports, for the 19 instances that can be rebuilt
(the BHOSLIB instances are excluded; see below), the kernel left under the 60\,s
run's own rule (kernelization limited to the share $\kappa=0.3$ of the budget),
the kernel at a fixed point, and the result of running the exact engine of
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} on the whole input for 120\,s (the engine is
\textsc{Cascade}\,\texttt{--exact}: the same reductions, then branch-and-reduce).
These runs were made on a different, shared machine from the main tables, with
three processes at a time, so kernelization times are only indicative.

\input{tables/kernels}

\emph{Kernel size is bimodal.} Six instances reduce to an empty kernel and a
seventh to 126 vertices; on these, kernelization alone is optimal, and direct
exact solving proves it in under 1.3\,s. For them region moves have nothing to
do, and the ties in Table~\ref{tab:quality-social} are exactly what one expects.
Road networks, random geometric graphs, web graphs and \texttt{as-skitter} keep
$0.3$--$4\%$ of their vertices (686 to 67\,274), and the Delaunay graphs keep
$85\%$. None of the 12
instances with a non-trivial kernel is solved by the exact engine in 120\,s
(\texttt{del18} and \texttt{del20} overran the limit because the reduction pass
is not interruptible; \texttt{soc-pokec} produced no output in 743\,s for the
same reason). So the premise of a hard residual kernel holds on 12 of the 19
instances, and does not on the other seven. Table~\ref{tab:exact} shows what
other exact solvers do on the smaller ones.

\emph{Kernel size does not predict the margin.} Across the 19 instances the
Spearman correlation between the retained fraction and the relative margin over
the best baseline is $\rho=0.14$ ($p=0.56$); omitting the three Delaunay graphs
it is $-0.57$ ($p=0.02$). All three strict wins have a kernel of about $85\%$.
But \texttt{soc-pokec}, whose capped kernel is $73\%$, is a loss, and the
instances with $0.3$--$4\%$ kernels are ties or small losses. A large kernel is
therefore not sufficient. What the three wins share beyond kernel size is that
they are planar, of bounded degree (at most 22) and generated by one mechanism;
the benchmark does not contain a second family with that property, and
Section~\ref{sec:delaunay} tests independent samples of it.

\emph{Budget caps change what ``kernel'' means.} For \texttt{com-lj} and
\texttt{soc-pokec} the cap of $0.3B=18$\,s stops kernelization early on this
machine, and where it stops depends on the speed of the run: three runs of the
same configuration left \texttt{com-lj} with $3544$, $212\,739$ (reported in
Table~\ref{tab:kernels}) and $899\,607$ vertices (the last with three processes
sharing the machine), against $1781$ at a fixed point after 24\,s;
\texttt{soc-pokec} is left with $73$--$75\%$ against $42\%$ after 332\,s. The
residual a budgeted run searches is thus a function of the budget and the
machine as much as of the graph, and a statement that ``\texttt{com-lj} reduces
to a few thousand vertices'' holds only if kernelization is allowed to finish.
On the machine of the main tables, where kernelization of \texttt{com-lj} took
14\,s in an earlier measurement, the cap does not bind.

\subsection{Seed study}

The solvers compared here are randomised, so we repeated a subset of ten
instances with five seeds each. Table~\ref{tab:seeds} reports the best and mean
size, and Figure~\ref{fig:seeds} the spread.

The single-seed picture holds, and no more than it. On best-of-five,
computed on relative gaps with Holm correction over four comparisons,
\textsc{Cascade} is significantly ahead of OnlineMIS ($p=0.0078$, adjusted
$0.047$) and indistinguishable from ReduMIS ($p=0.81$), NuMVC ($0.14$) and FastVC
($0.12$); on the mean over seeds the verdicts are the same (OnlineMIS $p=0.002$;
the others above $0.27$), with \textsc{Cascade} ahead of OnlineMIS on all ten
instances. NearLinear and LinearTime are deterministic and were not repeated.

The figure also shows a limitation. On every geometric, road and web instance
our solver's five runs collapse to a line: the spread is at most $0.024\%$, and
on two instances all five seeds return the same value. On the BHOSLIB instances
it widens by two orders of magnitude, to $2.5\%$ on \texttt{frb40-19-1} and
$2.0\%$ on \texttt{frb53-24-1}. It is not the most variable solver there ---
OnlineMIS spreads $5.1\%$ and $3.9\%$ on those two instances --- but it is
markedly less stable than ReduMIS and FastVC, which return the same value on
every seed of both, and on \texttt{frb40-19-1} that value is 40 against our 39
or 40. This is the same degeneracy discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:move} seen from another angle: when the region move cannot
find traction, what remains is the restart mechanism, and outcomes then depend
on which restart happens to land well. A practitioner running a single seed on a
dense instance should expect variance that the other families do not show.

Table~\ref{tab:longbudget} shows what each solver reaches by the end of its

The shapes differ more than the endpoints do. On \texttt{del18} and
\texttt{roadNet-PA} our solver reaches a value the others need most of the

\subsection{The long budget}
\label{sec:longbudget}

The main study gives every solver 60 seconds. That choice needs defending,
because a budget short enough to favour one convergence profile over another
would decide the comparison by itself. We therefore repeated four instances,
one per family, at ten times the budget. Table~\ref{tab:longbudget} gives the
result, and the comparison that matters is with the 60-second tables.

On three of the four instances the extra budget buys almost nothing.
\textsc{Cascade} and ReduMIS return \emph{exactly} the same value on
\texttt{roadNet-CA} and on \texttt{web-BerkStan} at 600 seconds as at 60, and on
\texttt{frb59-26-1} every solver moves by at most a single vertex. Those
instances are converged well inside the shorter budget, and nothing in the main
tables is an artefact of stopping early.

\texttt{del20} is the exception, and it qualifies our own headline. At 60
seconds \textsc{Cascade} leads ReduMIS there by 8500 vertices; at 600 seconds it
leads by 1550. ReduMIS gains 7417 vertices from the extra time and NuMVC 6227,
against 467 for \textsc{Cascade} and 390 for FastVC. The large gap to ReduMIS
that Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports on the largest Delaunay instance is
therefore substantially a statement about convergence rate rather than about
final quality: ReduMIS gets to a similar place, it simply takes an order of
magnitude longer to do it.

Two things survive that correction. \textsc{Cascade} still returns the best value
on \texttt{del20} at the long budget, 330\,533 against FastVC's 330\,192, and its
margin over the \emph{strongest} baseline is essentially unchanged by the extra
time --- 264 vertices at 60 seconds, 341 at 600. The advantage that the paper
claims over the best available alternative is robust to the budget. What is not
robust, and what we therefore do not claim, is the much larger gap to ReduMIS
specifically.

The table also shows the other side of the same coin, and it is not in our
favour. ReduMIS reaches its final value on \texttt{web-BerkStan} in 67 seconds
and on \texttt{roadNet-CA} in 69, matching or beating what \textsc{Cascade}
returns after ten times as long. On graphs whose kernel reduces to a small
fraction of the input, a solver that finishes kernelization and stops is simply
the better tool, and no amount of neighbourhood search recovers the difference.
This is the same boundary that Section~\ref{sec:move} predicts from the
structure of the move, seen from the direction of running time rather than
solution quality.

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The method exposes several tunable parameters, and a reader is entitled to ask
whether the results depend on having found good values for them.
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} varies four of them one at a time over a wide range,
holding the rest at their defaults, on three instances from different families.
The comparison that makes the numbers interpretable is with the seed-to-seed
spread of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}: a parameter whose whole range moves the
answer by less than the noise between two seeds is not a parameter the user has
to think about.

On the two large sparse instances that is what happens. Over ranges spanning a
factor of seven to sixteen, \texttt{kernel\_share}, \texttt{lns\_start\_free}
and \texttt{lns\_slice} move the result by at most $0.013\%$ on \texttt{del18}
and $0.001\%$ on \texttt{web-Stanford}, against a seed spread of $0.024\%$ on
that family. On \texttt{web-Stanford} two of them are exactly flat: every value of
\texttt{kernel\_share} from $0.1$ to $0.7$, and every value of
\texttt{lns\_slice} from $0.01$ to $0.2$, returns the same 163\,375.

One parameter is genuinely sensitive, and we report it as such.
\texttt{restart\_idle\_dives} moves \texttt{del18} by $0.081\%$, three times the
seed spread, and the shape is informative: 82\,592 at 50, 82\,639 at 100, 82\,659
at the default 200, then 82\,655 at both 400 and 800. Restarting too eagerly
costs real quality, while restarting too lazily costs almost nothing. The
default sits at the top of that curve rather than on its slope, but a reader
porting this method to a new family should know that this is the one dial worth
checking, and that the safe direction to err in is upwards.

The dense instance is where this design runs out of resolution, and we say so
rather than reading meaning into it. The spreads there are $2.5\%$ to $5\%$, but
every value in the block is 38, 39 or 40, and the seed study already reports a
$2.5\%$ spread on this family from seed alone. A single run per setting cannot
separate a parameter effect from that noise. What can be said is bounded: no
setting we tried collapses performance, and \texttt{lns\_start\_free}$=8$ returns
40 where the default returns 39 --- which is either a better setting or a luckier
seed, and this experiment cannot tell which.

\paragraph{Initial number of released vertices} The online rule adapts $k$, but
it starts from $k_0=4$, and a reader may ask whether that start matters.
Table~\ref{tab:ksweep} varies $k_0$ from $1$ to $1024$, a factor of $1024$, on

$82\,641$--$82\,660$ ($0.023\%$, the same as the seed-to-seed spread of
$0.024\%$ reported above), and on \texttt{web-Stanford} and \texttt{roadNet-PA}

The rule forgets its start where it matters: on \texttt{del18} the value of $k$
at the end of the run is $80$--$128$ for every $k_0$, and on \texttt{roadNet-PA}
it is $256$ for five of six. On \texttt{web-Stanford} and \texttt{rgg16} the
final $k$ does depend on $k_0$ ($128$--$1024$ and $32$--$512$), but the result does
not, which says that the objective is flat in $k$ there.

\subsection{Exact solvers}
\label{sec:exact}

The method proposed here is a heuristic, and the question of how far its answers
sit from optimal is separate from how they compare with other heuristics. To
place them we ran three exact solvers and one integer-programming solver on the
instances small enough to admit them: the branch-and-reduce solver built from
our own components (\textsc{Cascade}-exact, the sub-solver of
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} applied to the whole graph), VCSolver
\citep{akiba2016}, the winner of the vertex cover track of PACE 2019
\citep{pace2019,hespe2020}, and HiGHS on the natural
integer program. Table~\ref{tab:exact} reports the results.

Three conclusions follow, and only the first is favourable to us. On the
instances that reduce almost completely --- \texttt{ca-AstroPh},
\texttt{ca-CondMat}, \texttt{email-Enron} --- all four prove optimality, and our
sub-solver is the fastest of them --- by a factor of 25 to 40 over HiGHS, three
to five over VCSolver, and a more modest 1.1 to 1.4 over the PACE portfolio ---
which is the property
the region move depends on: the sub-problems it generates are of exactly this
shape, and at $10^3$ to $10^4$ moves per second (Table~\ref{tab:regional}) they must each be solved in a fraction
of a millisecond.

Second, our exact solver is not competitive as an exact solver. On the BHOSLIB
family the PACE 2019 portfolio proves the optimum of \texttt{frb30-15-1},
\texttt{frb35-17-1} and \texttt{frb40-19-1} in under four seconds each, while
ours returns 25, 28 and 30 against the true 30, 35 and 40 and proves nothing.
It is built to be restarted millions of times on graphs of a few dozen vertices,
not to prove optimality on a dense graph of a thousand, and the table shows the
cost of that choice. Where a proof is wanted on instances of this kind, the
portfolio approach is the right tool.

Third, the integer-programming route is not a usable baseline at this scale, and
we record its failures rather than omitting it. HiGHS matches the optimum on the
three easy instances but exceeds the 60-second budget on most others, taking up
to 360\,s; on \texttt{frb45-21-1} through \texttt{frb53-24-1} it returns a single
vertex, on \texttt{rgg18} none at all, and on \texttt{del18} and
\texttt{web-Stanford} it emits no solution we could verify. On
\texttt{email-Enron} it reports optimality but the solution it writes has 22\,253
vertices against the 22\,255 that the other three solvers prove optimal and that
our verifier confirms; we therefore treat its optimality claims as unreliable
and its numbers as a lower bound on what a tuned commercial solver would do.
This is a statement about the formulation at this size, not about HiGHS: the
natural model has one variable per vertex and one constraint per edge, which for
the larger instances in the benchmark means tens of millions of constraints.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports four variants on seven instances spanning the
families: \textsc{no-lns} disables region moves entirely, leaving kernelization
plus the greedy dive and its restarts; \textsc{dive} replaces the region move
with the decision-space move described in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}; \textsc{no-pert}
removes the perturbation step; and \textsc{no-lp} removes the
Nemhauser--Trotter LP reduction. These are separate runs from the main study, so
the full-solver column differs from Table~\ref{tab:quality-geometric} by a few
vertices; the comparison is within the table.

The region move is the component that carries the result. Removing it costs
$1.2\%$ on \texttt{del18} and $1.4\%$ on \texttt{del16} --- an order of magnitude
more than the margin the full solver holds over the strongest baseline on those
instances --- and $32.5\%$ on \texttt{frb40-19-1}, where the fallback is reduced
to a restarting greedy. Summed over the seven instances the loss is 1577
vertices. Nothing else in the solver comes close to that.

The other three results are negative, and we report them as such. The
decision-space move recovers almost none of the gap: on \texttt{del18},
\texttt{roadNet-PA} and \texttt{web-Stanford} it returns exactly the value
obtained with no neighbourhood move at all (81\,639, 533\,385 and 163\,369), so
on those instances it contributes nothing whatever. It is the move we designed
first, and the ablation is what showed it to be worthless; only on the dense
instances does it recover part of the loss. It survives in the implementation
as an option, not as a default.

Perturbation and the LP reduction are both within noise on this benchmark, and
on the family where the margin lives the shipped configuration is not the best
of the variants: without perturbation and restarts \texttt{del16} returns 20\,656
against 20\,648, and without the LP reduction \texttt{del18} returns 82\,666
against 82\,661. Both differences are of the order of the seed spread
($0.024\%$), so they are not evidence that the components hurt, but neither
are they evidence that they help.
Removing perturbation changes the summed total by $+2$ vertices, and hurts only
\texttt{frb40-19-1}, by two. Removing the LP reduction changes it by $+8$, and
on \texttt{frb59-26-1} the solver without it returns 57 rather than 56 --- which
is the value ReduMIS reaches and we do not. We keep both because each is cheap
and each is standard, and because a seven-instance ablation is too small to
conclude that they never help; but we have no evidence from these runs that
either pays for itself, and a reader looking to reimplement the method should
start with the region move and add the rest only if measurement justifies it.

\subsection{Exact against heuristic repair, and regional reductions}
\label{sec:regional}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes whole components; it does not separate the two
ingredients the method rests on, namely that the region is solved \emph{exactly}
and that the reductions run \emph{inside} the region. Table~\ref{tab:regional}
does, under one implementation, one region-selection rule (the walk of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade}), one budget and three seeds. The two variants
change only the sub-solve: \emph{heuristic repair} applies the root reduction
pass to the region and then a single greedy pass (lowest degree first), with no
branching and the same acceptance test $|T|\ge|F|$; \emph{no regional
reductions} runs the exact branch-and-bound on the raw region. In both, the
online rule for $k$ is the same code; for heuristic repair a sub-solve is
treated as ``solved'' whenever the region is below the size cap $\rho$, since
there is no proof signal to react to.

\input{tables/regional}

Both ingredients matter on the instances where the search has room to improve.
On \texttt{del18}, heuristic repair loses 143 vertices and removing the regional
reductions loses 210, against 1018 for removing region moves altogether; on
\texttt{del16}, 31, 50 and 269; on \texttt{roadNet-PA}, 20, 54 and 252. Exact
repair with reductions is the best of the four on every instance and the others
are never ahead (the two instances that reduce nearly completely, \texttt{rgg16}
and \texttt{com-amazon}, show no difference). So of the loss from removing
region moves, exact repair and regional reductions each recover most of it and
neither alone is the whole.

The process columns show why. Without reductions the sub-solver cannot afford
large regions: the online rule settles at $k=16$--$32$ released vertices and
regions of $36$--$53$ vertices on the four instances where it matters, against
$k=64$--$256$ and regions of $89$--$203$ vertices on the Delaunay and road
instances in the full configuration, which the root reduction pass collapses to
$5$--$17$ live vertices (a factor of $11$--$19$). With heuristic repair the rule has
no brake and $k$ climbs to $512$--$1024$, so a single greedy pass is applied to
regions of $177$--$589$ vertices, of which $33$--$137$ survive reduction. The share of
sub-solves proved optimal is close to $100\%$ in the full configuration on all
five instances, which is by construction: a sub-solve that fails halves $k$, so
the rule keeps the proved share high and the information is in the \emph{size}
of the region it can hold. The move rate is $1.3\times10^3$ to $8.4\times10^3$
region moves per second in the full configuration (1787 on \texttt{del18},
i.e.\ $560\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ per move; the earlier figure of $543\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ is
from the main-table machine).

\subsection{Independent Delaunay graphs}
\label{sec:delaunay}

All three strict wins of Table~\ref{tab:quality-geometric} are on one sample of one
generator, so the obvious question is whether they are a property of Delaunay
graphs or of that sample. Table~\ref{tab:delaunay} runs \textsc{Cascade} on six
independent samples at $2^{16}$ and $2^{18}$ points (generator seeds 1--6; seed 1
is the graph of the main benchmark) and on the public DIMACS10
\texttt{delaunay\_n16}, \texttt{n18} and \texttt{n20}, three solver seeds each
(two for seeds 4--6).

\input{tables/delaunay}

\emph{This experiment does not test the comparison.} The baselines were not run
on these graphs, because building and running them was outside what was done for
this revision, so Table~\ref{tab:delaunay} says nothing about whether
\textsc{Cascade} is ahead of ReduMIS or FastVC on a new Delaunay graph. What it
does show is how much a one-graph margin can mean. The mean over solver seeds
varies by $50.8$ vertices ($0.25\%$) across the six $2^{16}$ samples and by $84.5$
($0.10\%$) across the six $2^{18}$ samples, while the spread over solver seeds on
a fixed graph is at most 11 and 13. The between-sample range is thus several
times larger than the margins claimed on the seed-1 graph ($0.03\%$ and
$0.09\%$), and the seed-1 graph sits near the bottom of the range for $2^{16}$
(20\,648 against a mean of $20\,669$). A margin of that size measured on one
graph is within the variation between graphs, so it can only be interpreted as
a paired difference on the same graph, which is how Table~\ref{tab:quality-geometric}
reports it; it cannot be extrapolated to the family without paired runs on
more samples.

\subsection{Replication on different hardware}
\label{sec:replication}

A computational study is only as convincing as its independence from the machine
it was run on. We therefore repeated the comparison on a second, unrelated
platform: virtual machines with two Intel Xeon cores at 2.20\,GHz and 12\,GB of
memory, against the four-core workstation used for the main tables. Every solver
was rebuilt from source on that platform by the provisioning script included
with our code, so nothing is carried over except the source.

Two properties of the replication are worth stating explicitly. First, all
solvers for a given instance were run on the same virtual machine, so each row
of the replication table is internally consistent; only different rows may come
from different machines, and those machines are of identical specification.
Splitting a single instance's solvers across machines would have made the
comparison meaningless, since the budget is a wall-clock budget and the amount
of work that fits into it depends on the processor. Second, the two largest
instances were excluded, because 12\,GB of memory is not enough for them; the
replication therefore covers 25 of the 27 instances.

Table~\ref{tab:replication} reports the outcome. The weak statement that
survives is that on each of the 25 instances the set of solvers attaining the
best value on the replication machine intersects the set attaining it on the
original machine. That statement alone does not say that the \emph{ranking} is
preserved, so we also compared every pair of the seven solvers on every
instance, 519 pairs in total with a value from both platforms. The sign of the
difference between the two solvers (better, equal, worse) is the same on both
platforms for 498 of them ($96.0\%$). In the remaining 21 the pair is equal on
one platform and strictly ordered on the other, and \emph{in none of the 519 does
the order reverse}. Restricted to \textsc{Cascade} against each baseline, 9 of
149 pairs change from equal to strict or back. The comparisons in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} are therefore not artefacts of the machine to the
extent that two machines of this class can show.

The individual values move a little, and the table shows where. The two
deterministic solvers, reducing-peeling and near-linear kernelization, return
\emph{identical} values on all 25 instances, which is the control: it confirms that
the instances, the conversions and the verifier are the same on both platforms,
so any difference elsewhere comes from the solver rather than from the pipeline.
The five randomised solvers differ by a fraction of a percent on average, and
what difference there is concentrates on BHOSLIB, where the optima are small
enough that a one-vertex change is one to two percent. Away from that family our
own solver returns the same value on both machines on every road, web and social
instance, and differs by an average of $0.016\%$ on the geometric family.

Two caveats belong with this. The replication machine is slower, so a solver
that spends its whole budget does less work within it; that is visible as the
larger spreads for the vertex cover local search solvers, which have no
reduction phase to fall back on, and most sharply as NuMVC exceeding the cutoff
altogether on \texttt{roadNet-CA}, an instance it completed on the faster
machine. And a fixed wall-clock budget on two machines
of different speed is not the same experiment twice --- it is the same
\emph{protocol} twice, which is what a practitioner choosing a solver under a
deadline actually faces.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Measurement} All timings are wall-clock and include reading the
instance. Kernelization is bounded by a share of the budget but is not
interruptible in the middle of a rule, so our own solver can overrun slightly;
Table~\ref{tab:budget} reports the distribution for every solver, and the
baseline that overruns most is the one our comparison is least favourable to.

\paragraph{Two machines} The additions of this revision (kernel statistics,
whole-input exact solves, the regional ablation, the sweep over $k_0$ and the
independent Delaunay graphs) were run on a four-core Intel Xeon at 2.80\,GHz
under Ubuntu 24.04 with GCC 13.3, with three single-threaded processes at a
time. The main tables were produced on the machine of Section~\ref{sec:setup-machine}.
The two are not interchangeable: kernelization under a time cap, in particular,
depends on speed, as the three measurements of \texttt{com-lj} in
Section~\ref{sec:kernels} show. Tables~\ref{tab:kernels}--\ref{tab:ksweep} are
internally consistent but are not directly comparable with the main tables in
absolute terms.

\paragraph{Unverified values} The reference implementations of reducing-peeling
report a solution size without emitting the corresponding set, so those values
cannot be checked by our verifier and are taken on trust. All other values in
this paper were re-derived from an emitted solution.

\paragraph{Instance provenance} The Delaunay and random geometric instances of
the main benchmark are generated, reproducible from the seed in the generator we
distribute, and are not byte-identical to the DIMACS10 graphs whose names they
resemble; Section~\ref{sec:delaunay} therefore adds six independent generator
seeds and the public DIMACS10 \texttt{delaunay\_n16}, \texttt{n18} and
\texttt{n20}. Checksums of every downloaded and generated file are distributed
with the source.

\paragraph{Randomisation} Our solver, ReduMIS, OnlineMIS, NuMVC and FastVC are
randomised. The main table reports a single seed per instance; the seed study
repeats a subset with five seeds and reports both the best and the mean, and the
statistical test is computed on per-instance aggregates.

\paragraph{Failures} Two solvers failed to return any solution on some
instance within the cutoff, which we report as a failure rather than excluding
the instance. Excluding them would have removed exactly the cases that
distinguish the solvers.

\paragraph{Implementation maturity} The baselines are mature codebases. NuMVC
and FastVC have been refined by their authors and others since 2013 and 2015
respectively, and the KaMIS solvers likewise; ours is a research implementation
written for this paper. A comparison of wall-clock results under a fixed budget
cannot separate the merit of an algorithm from the quality of the code that runs
it, and the reader should not read our numbers as though it could.

We nevertheless report the comparison as measured, for three reasons. The
strongest baseline on this benchmark, ReduMIS, is as heavily engineered as the
vertex cover solvers, and it is the one against which we claim the least: no
test in this paper separates us from it. Excluding the mature implementations
would remove exactly the solvers a practitioner would otherwise choose. And the
bias runs against us where we win and for us where we lose, which is the less
convenient direction: our margins over NuMVC on the large sparse instances ---
46\,054 vertices on \texttt{roadNet-CA}, 18\,645 on \texttt{del20} --- would only
be larger if implementation quality were equalised, while our one- and two-vertex
deficits on BHOSLIB might narrow.

We do not, therefore, attribute the BHOSLIB result to implementation maturity.
Section~\ref{sec:move} gives a structural reason for it --- on a dense graph the
region is either empty or the whole graph, so the move degenerates --- and the
convergence traces support that reading: NuMVC reaches the final value there in
$0.2$ seconds, which is not the signature of a better-tuned inner loop but of a
search whose neighbourhood suits the instance.

\paragraph{Build differences between the two platforms} The replication
machines rebuilt the solver from source, and that source had by then acquired
the tracing used for Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}, which the machine used for
the main tables did not carry. The two builds are therefore not bit-identical,
and the replication in Section~\ref{sec:replication} varies build as well as
machine. We measured the size of that confound rather than assuming it away:
running both builds on the same machine, with the same seed and budget, they
return identical values on \texttt{del18}, \texttt{rgg16}, \texttt{web-Stanford}
and \texttt{roadNet-PA}, and differ by five vertices out of 20\,647 on
\texttt{del16}, which is $0.024\%$. That is the same order as the seed-to-seed
spread we report for the geometric family, and an order of magnitude below any
difference the replication is used to argue about. It does not affect the
ranking, which is what that section claims; but the per-instance agreement
counts in Table~\ref{tab:replication} should be read as bounded by it rather
than as pure machine effects.

\paragraph{Baselines not run} The baselines were published between 2012 and
2019. We did not run more recent solvers. InfVC \citep{sun2025infvc} is the
task-matched candidate: a local search for unweighted vertex cover on massive
graphs that compares against successors of FastVC. The comparison would require
building it under the same instance formats, hardware and timing rules as the
other solvers, which was not done for this paper, and published numbers obtained
under different conditions cannot stand in for it. Until that is done the claims
about ranking are claims against the 2012--2019 generation of solvers, and in
particular the observation that FastVC has the lowest mean gap of the seven
suggests the vertex-cover local search line deserves a current baseline. The
dynamic framework of \citet{borowitz2025} was likewise not run, so the
comparison with it is a comparison of constructions (Table~\ref{tab:frameworks}),
not of performance. The weighted solvers cited in Section~\ref{sec:related}
address a different problem.

\paragraph{How narrow the positive result is} Our solver is strictly ahead of
every baseline on three of 27 instances, all Delaunay graphs from a single
generator seed. On thirteen instances it ties the best value and on eleven it is
behind. The Wilcoxon tests of Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon} are computed over
relative gaps with Holm correction; they find it ahead of OnlineMIS,
NearLinear and LinearTime, which degrade on large sparse graphs, and find no
significant difference from ReduMIS, NuMVC or FastVC. By mean relative gap
it is third of seven. The claim that survives is therefore narrow:
on planar bounded-degree graphs whose kernel stays large it is ahead of
every solver we ran, by a margin of $0.03$--$0.09\%$; on graphs that reduce away
it is level with the best; on dense instances it is behind. Section~\ref{sec:delaunay}
tests the Delaunay claim on independent samples. We know of no public
collection of other large-kernel families, and we make no claim about them.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We studied a region move for the maximum independence set problem: release part
of an incumbent, collect the vertices that become eligible, and re-solve the
region by branch-and-reduce. The move is admissible and monotone for any
sub-solve that returns a set at least as large as the one released. Its region
construction is that of \citet{borowitz2025}; our contribution is its use as the
inner move of a static solver, with reductions applied inside the region, an online
rule for the number of released vertices, and an empirical account of when it
helps.

The account is mixed. Against six solvers from 2012--2019 on 27 instances, the
method is strictly ahead on three Delaunay graphs from one generator seed, level on
13 and behind on 11; it is not significantly different from ReduMIS, NuMVC or
FastVC, and by mean relative gap it is third of seven. Inside the
method, exact repair and reductions inside the region each recover a substantial
part of the loss from removing region moves, and the reductions are what allow the
regions to be an order of magnitude larger. The residual kernel is large on 12
of the 19 instances measured, but its size does not predict where the method
wins; independent Delaunay samples vary more between graphs than the margin
claimed on one. The claim we make is therefore narrow: on planar bounded-degree
graphs with large kernels the method is ahead of the 2012--2019 solvers on the
one graph where it was compared; on the rest of the sparse benchmark it is level
with the best solver or within $0.06\%$ of it, the largest deficits being on web
graphs and \texttt{soc-pokec}; on dense instances it is behind.

Three things are needed before a stronger claim. A current baseline,
for instance InfVC, run under the same rules; paired baseline runs on the
independent Delaunay samples; and a second large-kernel family. The region
definition and Theorem~\ref{thm:region} carry over to the weighted problem by
replacing cardinality with weight.

\section{Instance provenance}
\label{app:provenance}

Table~\ref{tab:provenance} records where each instance came from, so that the
benchmark set can be rebuilt exactly. Three points deserve emphasis, because each
is a way in which two studies can report different numbers for what they believe
is the same graph.

First, the real-world instances are distributed as directed or multiply-listed
edge lists. We read every file as undirected, discard self-loops and duplicate
edges, and renumber the vertices consecutively; the $n$ and $m$ in the table are
the values after this normalisation, and they are what our solver and every
baseline receive. Isolated vertices are retained rather than dropped. Removing $q$ isolated
vertices lowers the independence number by exactly $q$, so a study that drops
them reports values smaller by $q$ than one that keeps them, and the two are not
comparable unless $q$ is stated.

Second, the BHOSLIB instances are published in two forms, and the distinction is
easy to miss. The Network Repository archive ships the \emph{clique} form, whose
independence number is the size of a Model-RB domain rather than the advertised
$\alpha$: on \texttt{frb30-15-1} that form has 83\,198 edges and every solver we
tried returned exactly 15. The form used here, and the one the published
$\alpha$ values refer to, is its complement, with 17\,827 edges and $\alpha=30$.
Our converter takes the complement explicitly. A study that omits this step is
not solving a hard instance at all, and its BHOSLIB numbers cannot be compared
with anyone else's.

Third, the geometric instances are generated rather than downloaded. They come
from \texttt{tools/gen\_instances.py} at seed 1: the Delaunay instances are the
Delaunay triangulation of $2^{k}$ uniform points in the unit square, and the
random geometric instances connect points within the radius that gives an
expected average degree of 8. The generator is deterministic given the seed, so
the instances in the table are reproducible, but they are \emph{not} the DIMACS
implementation-challenge graphs of the same names, and the two should not be
compared.
